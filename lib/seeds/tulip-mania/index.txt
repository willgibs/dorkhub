Tulip mania was a frenzy of buying and reselling tulip bulbs that peaked in the towns of the Dutch Republic in the winter of 1636–37 and broke in the first week of February 1637. It is often called the first recorded speculative bubble, yet much of the famous story was written afterwards by moralists, and historians still argue over how mad it really was.

## From Ottoman gardens to Leiden

Wild tulips grow across the mountains of Central Asia, and Ottoman gardeners prized them before western Europe did; in the 1720s Istanbul would have a tulip craze of its own, the [[Tulip Era]]. The Habsburg envoy [[Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq]] described them in letters from his embassy to Suleiman the Magnificent, and is often credited, perhaps too generously, with sending the first bulbs west. The botanist [[Carolus Clusius]] planted tulips in the new [[botanical garden at Leiden|Hortus Botanicus Leiden]] in 1593, and thieves twice raided his garden for them.

^ Tulip and turban began as the same word, Persian dulband; the Turks themselves called the flower lale.

## Flames, feathers and a virus

The bulbs that fetched fortunes were "broken" tulips, their petals feathered and flamed with a second colour on white or yellow. Nobody knew the pattern was a disease: the [[Tulip breaking virus]], spread by aphids such as the [[Green peach aphid]]. The infection also weakened the bulb, so the most coveted flowers multiplied the slowest, and no one could predict which plain tulip would break next. Only in the late 1920s did the mycologist Dorothy Cayley prove that breaking was an infection.

^ A pamphlet dialogue about the mania advised grafting half a broken bulb onto half a plain one to make it break, a trick that could genuinely spread the virus.

The most famous broken tulip was [[Semper Augustus]], white flamed with carmine. In the mid-1620s a chronicler reported that only about a dozen bulbs existed, all owned by one collector, who refused 3,000 guilders for a single bulb. Because a tulip blooms for only a week or so, dealers sold from painted catalogues, the [[tulip books|Tulip book]].

## Trading in wind

Bulbs spend autumn to early summer in the ground, so for most of the year a buyer could purchase only a promise. From mid-1636 dealers met in tavern clubs called colleges, with fixed bidding rituals and a small fee, wine money, on each deal. The Dutch called it [[windhandel|Windhandel]], trading in wind: no bulb was delivered, no deposit was asked, and one contract could be resold several times. From November the trade spread to common bulbs sold by the pound, whose prices rose as much as twentyfold in January 1637.

Then the buyers stopped coming. Sellers were left holding promises that would not be kept, and no one could say whether a deal struck over wine in a tavern bound anyone at all. Settling the contracts dragged on for more than a year, and many were never honoured.

::: timeline
Early Feb 1637 :: A Haarlem auctioneer reportedly cuts prices but finds no buyers
Same week :: Alkmaar sells an innkeeper's bulbs for his orphans: about 90,000 guilders
Late Feb 1637 :: Florists' delegates in Amsterdam propose letting recent buyers quit for 10%
Apr 1637 :: Holland's provincial authorities decline to rule, leaving disputes to towns
May 1638 :: Haarlem lets buyers cancel by paying 3.5% of the price
:::

## How the legend grew

Moralists struck at once. Pamphlets mocked the florists, and within a few years Jan Brueghel the Younger painted them as monkeys in fine clothes, weighing bulbs, facing a judge and being carried to the grave, in the Flemish tradition of [[singerie|Singerie]]. Two centuries later the Scottish journalist Charles Mackay set the tulips beside the [[South Sea Bubble]] in [[his 1841 book on popular delusions|Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds]], complete with a sailor who ate a costly bulb, taking it for an onion. His version became the one everyone knows.

Historians who returned to the archives tell a smaller story. The economist Peter Garber argued in 1989 that rare new bulbs routinely started dear and cheapened as they multiplied, leaving only the final month, when common bulbs soared and crashed, as a possible bubble. The historian Anne Goldgar, reading notaries' records, argued in 2007 that the deeper damage was to trust and honour. Whether January's buyers were chasing a [[greater fool|Greater fool theory]] or betting they could walk away from their contracts cheaply is still argued.

::: myth
Myth :: The crash bankrupted crowds of ordinary Dutch people and sank the economy.
Actually :: Trading involved a small circle of well-off merchants and craftsmen; archives show fewer than half a dozen in trouble, and Dutch prosperity carried on.
:::
