In the winter of 1636–37, in the taverns of Haarlem and other Dutch towns, men gathered around tables to buy and sell tulips that were buried in the cold ground. The trade was done on paper, in the form of promises to deliver a bulb after the plants had flowered and been lifted in summer. Prices for the most coveted varieties rose steeply week after week, and then, on a single day in early February 1637, buyers failed to appear at an auction in Haarlem. Within days the market for the rarest contracts had folded, leaving a story that has been retold for four centuries as the model of a speculative bubble.

The tulip itself had arrived only recently. Bulbs reached the Low Countries from the Ottoman world in the later sixteenth century, and the botanist [[Charles de l'Écluse]], working at Leiden from 1593, planted them in the university garden and helped spread them among collectors. By the 1630s, riding the wealth of the [[Dutch Golden Age]], tulips were a status flower for merchants who had money to spend and gardens to display it in. What made some bulbs worth fortunes was a quirk of biology: the "broken" tulips, with flames and feathers of colour streaking the petals, were prized above plain ones.

^ The beauty was a disease. Broken colours come from a virus spread by aphids, which weakens the bulb and makes the prized varieties hard to propagate.

## How the market worked

Early on, bulbs were bought the ordinary way, lifted from the ground in summer. As demand grew, trading moved into the rest of the year through [[Futures contract|forward contracts]] and, later, a rapid sale of the contracts themselves, often settled by notes rather than cash. Much of this took place in colleges, informal clubs meeting in inns, where small buyers could join for modest sums and trade by the day. Contemporaries called such business [[Windhandel]], "wind trade," because nothing tangible changed hands.

The prices quoted for the grandest bulbs are what made the episode famous. The variety [[Semper Augustus]], with white petals flamed in crimson, was the most celebrated, and its price was said to have reached about 5,500 guilders for a single bulb in 1637, in a period when a skilled craftsman might earn around 300 guilders a year. Such figures survive in pamphlets and records, though the biggest prices were often for contracts that were never fulfilled.

Summer 1636 :: Lifted bulbs sell openly; rare varieties already command large sums.
Autumn 1636 :: Trade in unplanted bulbs spreads through inns and colleges.
December 1636 :: Prices climb sharply, drawing in small buyers.
Early February 1637 :: Buyers fail to appear at a Haarlem auction; rare-bulb prices fall.
:::

## The crash, and what it really did

The pamphlets of 1637 describe ruin, but the evidence for widespread financial disaster is thin. The historian [[Anne Goldgar]], who searched Dutch archives for her 2007 book *Tulipmania*, found few people who were clearly bankrupted by the trade, and no sign that the Dutch economy was damaged. A plausible trigger for the sudden stop was an outbreak of [[Bubonic plague]] in the region, which may have thinned the crowds and frightened buyers. Authorities then debated what to do with contracts that debtors refused to honour; in 1638 the Haarlem courts and later the provincial government declined to enforce them as ordinary debts, and many were settled at a small fraction of the nominal price.

The legend owes much to moralising Dutch satirists and then, in 1841, to Charles Mackay's [[Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions|Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds]], which popularised the picture of a whole nation gone mad. It became the standard comparison for the [[South Sea Bubble]] and later manias, and the standard shorthand for a [[Bubble (economics)|bubble]]. Modern economists argue over whether prices were irrational at all, since the market for rare, slowly propagated bulbs may have had a logic of scarcity. Whatever the verdict, the Dutch economy simply carried on, and its bigger enterprises, such as the [[Dutch East India Company]], stayed far larger than any flower trade.
