The Enigma machine looks like a heavy typewriter in a wooden case, with a keyboard, a lamp board of 26 glowing letters, and a row of lettered wheels under a hinged lid. Press a key and a different letter lights up; type the lit letters out, and you have a ciphertext that a second Enigma, set identically, turns straight back into the original. Invented by the German engineer Arthur Scherbius, who patented it in 1918, it was first sold to businesses, then adopted by the German army, navy and air force from the late 1920s. Tens of thousands were built, and the Germans trusted it so completely that they sent their most sensitive wartime orders through it.

## Inside the box

The heart of the machine is a set of [[Rotor machine|rotors]]: discs wired internally so that each of the 26 contacts on one face connects to a scrambled contact on the other. A keypress sends current through three rotors, into a reflector that bounces it back through the same rotors by a different path, and out to a lamp. Before the current even enters the rotors, a [[Plugboard|plugboard]] on the front, added to the army model in 1930, swapped pairs of letters using cables. After every keypress the rightmost rotor steps forward one position, and now and then it nudges its neighbour, so the scrambling changes with every letter. The same letter typed twice in a row comes out as two different letters.

The numbers were staggering. Choice of rotors, their starting positions, their ring settings and the plugboard pairings gave roughly 10^23 daily keys for the standard army model. The naval versions added a fourth rotor and more rotors to choose from. Operators were given monthly codebooks listing the settings for each day, so every machine on a network started the day aligned.

The reflector gave Enigma its great convenience, since one setting both enciphers and deciphers. It also produced the flaw. Because current had to come back by a different route, no letter could ever be enciphered as itself: an A could become anything but A. That sounds trivial, but it is a leak of information, and it turned a vast search into a manageable one.

^ Because an Enigma was reciprocal, if the machine turned P into Q at some moment, it would turn Q into P at that same position.

## Breaking the unbreakable

The first break came before the war. In December 1932 the Polish mathematician [[Marian Rejewski]] of the [[Biuro Szyfrów]] in Warsaw treated Enigma's daily scrambling as a [[Permutation|permutation]] and used the way operators repeated their message key twice, plus a set of German documents supplied by French intelligence, to deduce the wiring of the rotors. The Poles then built devices to find daily settings. In July 1939, with war imminent, they shared everything with British and French intelligence at a meeting in the Pyry forest near Warsaw.

At [[Bletchley Park]], [[Alan Turing]] and Gordon Welchman designed the [[Bombe]], an electromechanical machine that tested possible rotor settings in parallel. It relied on a [[Crib (cryptanalysis)|crib]]: a guess at a phrase the message probably contained, such as a weather report or the words "Heil Hitler." This is a [[Known-plaintext attack|known-plaintext attack]], the same family of trick that let scholars crack [[Hieroglyphs]] by matching the royal names on the Rosetta Stone to their Greek translation. Here the never-itself rule did the heavy lifting: slide a crib along the ciphertext, and any alignment where a letter matches itself is impossible and is thrown out immediately.

::: timeline
1918 :: Scherbius patents the rotor cipher machine.
Dec 1932 :: Rejewski reconstructs the army Enigma's wiring.
Jul 1939 :: Poland hands its Enigma methods to Britain and France.
Mar 1940 :: First British Bombe, Victory, enters service.
Feb 1942 :: Naval four-rotor Enigma blacks out U-boat traffic.
Dec 1942 :: Bletchley breaks the new naval cipher again.
:::

## What the breaking changed

Decrypts were distributed under the codename [[Ultra (intelligence)|Ultra]], carefully disguised so the Germans would not suspect. Naval Enigma, used by the [[Kriegsmarine]] with extra rotors and tight procedures, was the hardest; its capture of codebooks from weather ships and U-boats such as U-110 helped keep Atlantic convoys supplied. Estimates of how much Ultra shortened the war vary, with some historians saying months and others up to two years, and it is not a settled figure.

Enigma was not the only German cipher. Higher-level strategic traffic used the [[Lorenz cipher]], a teleprinter system broken by different methods that led to Colossus. The Enigma story is also a lesson in human error: lazy operators chose predictable keys, and German confidence that the system was unbreakable kept them from changing it.

^ Enigma was never defeated by raw machine power alone; clerks' habits, such as sending messages ending in the same sign-off, supplied many cribs.
