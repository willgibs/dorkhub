A Fresnel lens is a thick lens sliced into concentric rings and flattened. Take an ordinary glass lens: most of its bulk does nothing except carry the curve from one surface to the other, and it absorbs light while adding weight. Cut away that bulk, keep only the curved faces as a series of stepped prisms, and the lens bends light almost identically at a fraction of the mass. In a [[Lighthouse]], this meant a lens taller than a person could be built, lifted, and turned by clockwork. In a modern pocket magnifier or a car's rear window, it means a sheet of plastic you can bend with your thumb.

## From a stepped idea to a working tower

The notion is older than Fresnel. In 1748 [[Buffon]] suggested grinding a lens from a single block in concentric steps, and the Marquis de Condorcet later proposed building one from separate pieces. Neither produced a practical lighthouse lens. The man who did was [[Augustin-Jean Fresnel]], a French civil engineer and a convinced advocate of the [[Wave theory of light]], who in 1819 joined the French lighthouse commission. Reflectors of polished silvered copper then lit most towers, lit by [[Argand lamp]] flames, and they lost most of what they gathered.

Fresnel's design, completed in 1822 and first lit at [[Cordouan Lighthouse]] in 1823, put a central bull's-eye lens at the lamp's height, ringed by concentric prisms above and below. The rings caught light that would otherwise have sprayed into the sky or the sea and bent it into a horizontal beam. Early lenses were made of many separate glass pieces mounted in a brass frame, because casting a single large stepped piece was beyond the glassworks of the day.

^ Fresnel was working on his lenses while also helping to overturn the particle theory of light, and he died in 1827 at 39, only a few years after his lighthouse work began.

::: steps
Lamp :: A single oil flame sits at the lens's focal point.
Central lens :: The bull's-eye catches light and bends it into a horizontal beam.
Prisms above and below :: Angled rings bend rays that would have escaped upward or downward.
Mirror prisms :: Outer rings reflect light by total internal reflection, adding the remaining rays.
Rotation :: Clockwork turns the assembly, sweeping the beam into flashes.
:::

## Why it worked so well

The outer rings of a mature Fresnel assembly are [[Catadioptric system|catadioptric]]: they use refraction and reflection together. A ray entering the prism bends once, strikes the back face at an angle past the critical value, and reflects totally with essentially no loss, then bends again on the way out. That fix let a lens collect light across nearly 180 degrees of vertical arc, far more than any conventional lens could.

Rotating the assembly with a weight-driven clockwork turned the fixed beam into a flash, giving each tower its own signature of timing. Lenses were sorted into orders by focal length, from the first order (about 920 mm) down to the sixth, and a rare "hyperradiant" size above the first order was built for the busiest coasts. The [[Makapuʻu Point Lighthouse]] in Hawaii holds one of these, roughly 3.7 metres tall and 12 tons of glass and brass. British makers such as [[Chance Brothers]] supplied lenses to Scottish engineers including [[Thomas Stevenson]], while the American [[Lighthouse Board]] adopted the system in 1852 after long official resistance.

## Beyond the tower

Flatten the rings onto a sheet and the same geometry shrinks into everyday life. An [[Overhead projector]] uses a large Fresnel lens, usually under its glass plate, to gather lamp light and send it up toward the projection head. Car taillights, solar concentrators, and the thin plastic magnifier cards in wallets all rely on the same idea. The trade-off is that the steps scatter some light and soften the image, which is why Fresnel lenses are poor for photography but excellent for throwing or collecting light.

The wave-optics work that gave Fresnel his name also produced his equations for reflection and the [[Mark-Louis Poisson spot]], a bright point predicted at the centre of a circular shadow. That prediction, first used as an argument against his wave theory, was confirmed by experiment and helped win the argument for him.
