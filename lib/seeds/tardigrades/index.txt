Under a microscope, a tardigrade looks like a plump, translucent bear that has wandered into the wrong scale: eight clawed legs, a rounded body, and a head that sways as it feels its way across a film of water. Johann August Ephraim Goeze, a German pastor, described one in 1773 and called it the "little water bear"; the Italian naturalist [[Lazzaro Spallanzani]] named the group Tardigrada, "slow walkers," in 1776. Adults mostly measure under a millimetre, yet tardigrades live in the film of water around [[Moss]] and lichen, in sediments, on mountaintops and in the deep sea. They are animals in the full sense: a brain, a gut, muscles, and, in many species, a few hundred cells that never divide after hatching.

^ Despite the "slow walker" name, a tardigrade moves at roughly a few body lengths per minute; its fame comes from stillness, not speed.

## Dying without dying

The trick behind their reputation is [[Cryptobiosis]], a state in which metabolism falls to a level that is hard to detect at all. When the water around a tardigrade evaporates, the animal pulls in its legs, expels most of its water, and shrivels into a wrinkled barrel called a [[Tun state|tun]]. This form of [[Anhydrobiosis]] can last for years, and tuns have been revived after about a decade in dry storage, with reports of far longer survival under less rigorous conditions. Add water and, within hours, the animal rehydrates and walks away.

Tun formation takes preparation. Slow drying matters: tardigrades dried abruptly often fail, while those given time to adjust survive far better. Many species rely on production of sugars and protective proteins during the drying window, and the tun's shrunken geometry, with legs tucked into the body, limits water loss to the air.

::: steps
Drying begins :: Surrounding water evaporates and the animal contracts its body lengthwise.
Tun forms :: Legs retract and the body shrivels into a compact barrel.
Metabolism drops :: Activity falls to a level that is barely measurable.
Protection takes over :: Protective proteins and sugars stabilise cell structures.
Rehydration :: Added water restores the animal, often within hours.
:::

## What protects them

Early explanations pointed to [[Trehalose]], a sugar that shields cells in other dried organisms such as yeast and brine shrimp. Tardigrades turned out to be inconsistent on this point: some species make little of it, so something else must be at work. Attention shifted to proteins unique to tardigrades, including families of [[Intrinsically disordered proteins]] that lack a fixed shape and may form a glass-like matrix around cellular components as water leaves.

In 2016, Takekazu Kunieda's team in Tokyo reported a protein they named Dsup, the [[Damage suppressor protein]], from the species [[Ramazzottius varieornatus]]. It binds to DNA and appeared to reduce X-ray damage; human cells engineered to express it showed about 40 percent less damage in the experiment. The same year, a genome of [[Hypsibius exemplaris]] claimed a huge share of foreign genes through [[Horizontal gene transfer]], but later sequencing showed that figure was largely contamination, a cautionary tale about genome assembly.

^ Tardigrades share the group [[Ecdysozoa]], the moulting animals, with insects, spiders and roundworms; their closest relatives are probably velvet worms and arthropods.

## Tested in space

In September 2007, the European Space Agency's [[Foton-M3]] mission exposed dried tardigrades to open space for about ten days. Researchers led by Ingemar Jönsson in Sweden found that many survived the vacuum, and a subset survived solar ultraviolet radiation as well, with some going on to lay eggs. This was not the whole story: results varied by species and by exposure, and survival was never universal.

The lab record is a series of such limits. Dried tardigrades have survived temperatures near absolute zero, brief heating above 100 degrees Celsius, and pressures far beyond those at the ocean floor. These tolerances almost certainly evolved for ordinary hazards, such as drying puddles and freezing mountain moss, rather than for space; no tardigrade has ever needed to survive a vacuum. Active, hydrated animals are far more fragile, and they die like any other small animal in boiling water or sustained dryness.
