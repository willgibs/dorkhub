On a summer evening a thunderstorm cloud towers ten kilometres high, and somewhere inside it a spark begins that will run for kilometres through air, an excellent insulator. Within about a fifth of a second the bolt has carried tens of thousands of amperes, heating a channel only a few centimetres wide to roughly 30,000 °C, about five times the temperature of the Sun's visible surface. The air cannot expand fast enough, so it detonates outward as a shock wave: [[thunder]]. Earth is struck about 40 to 50 times every second, some 1.4 billion flashes a year, most of them never seen by anyone.

^ Most lightning never touches the ground. Roughly three in four flashes stay inside a cloud or leap between clouds.

## Charging a cloud

Inside a storm cloud, ice crystals, soft hail pellets called graupel, and supercooled droplets are tossed about by updrafts. When graupel and ice crystals collide and rebound, they swap tiny charges, a [[triboelectric effect|triboelectric]]-style process whose exact details are still debated. The common result is that lighter, positively charged crystals are carried to the top of the cloud while heavier graupel, negatively charged, sinks to the middle, with a smaller positive pocket often found near the base. The base of the cloud, now strongly negative, induces a positive charge in the ground beneath it, which piles up on hills, trees, towers and people.

Air is a poor conductor, and ordinary dry air should only break down at around three million volts per metre. Measurements inside storms find fields several times weaker than that, which is a genuine puzzle. Current hypotheses involve runaway electrons accelerated by cosmic-ray showers, and ice particles that sharpen the local field.

## Anatomy of a bolt

A strike happens in stages that the eye merges into one flash. First a faint, branching [[stepped leader|stepped leader]] descends from the cloud in jumps of about 50 metres, pausing for tens of microseconds between steps, feeling its way toward the ground. As it nears, streamers rise from pointed objects to meet it. The moment they connect, a [[return stroke]] surges back up the channel at roughly a third of the speed of light; this is the blinding part, and it is what heats the air.

Many bolts then repeat the cycle. A typical flash contains three to four strokes down the same channel, which is why lightning seems to flicker. The gaps are tens of milliseconds, just long enough for the eye to catch.

::: steps
Charging :: Colliding ice and graupel separate charge; the cloud base turns negative
Leader :: A faint negative channel steps downward in roughly 50 m jumps
Connection :: Streamers rise from the ground and meet the leader's tip
Return stroke :: A bright current wave races upward, heating air to about 30,000 °C
Thunder :: The superheated air expands explosively and rumbles outward
:::

## Strange cousins

Lightning leaves marks. Sand struck by a bolt can fuse into a branching glass tube called a [[fulgurite]], and skin and trees can bear fern-shaped [[Lichtenberg figure|Lichtenberg figures]]. Ash clouds from erupting volcanoes crackle with [[volcanic lightning]], generated by colliding charged particles rather than ice. Venezuela's [[Catatumbo lightning]] flashes on most nights of the year above where the Catatumbo River meets Lake Maracaibo.

Above storms, tall red [[Sprites (upper-atmospheric lightning)|sprites]] flicker briefly at altitudes of 50 to 90 kilometres, and satellites have recorded [[terrestrial gamma-ray flash]]es, bursts of gamma rays that storms emit, first detected in 1994. [[Ball lightning]], glowing spheres reported for centuries, remains unsettled: a few spectra have been recorded, but no reliable laboratory explanation exists.

The human toll is real but shrinking. Park ranger [[Roy Sullivan]] was struck seven times between 1942 and 1977 and survived them all. Benjamin Franklin's proposed [[lightning rod]] of the 1750s still protects buildings by giving the bolt an easy path to ground, and the glow of [[St. Elmo's fire]] on ship masts is the same electric field leaking quietly instead of striking.
