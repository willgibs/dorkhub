# The Great Emu War
> In 1932 three Australian soldiers took two machine guns into Western Australia's wheatbelt against some 20,000 emus, and the emus came out ahead.

The Great Emu War was the Australian Army's attempt, in November and December 1932, to cull the emus ravaging wheat farms in the [[Western Australian wheatbelt]]. The entire force was Major G. P. W. Meredith, a sergeant and a gunner of the Royal Australian Artillery, armed with two [[Lewis guns|Lewis gun]] and 10,000 rounds of ammunition. Facing them were an estimated 20,000 [[emus|Emu]], Australia's tallest native birds, spread across the farms around Campion, some 300 kilometres east of Perth. The press called it a war, and the birds came through it with their numbers barely dented.

## Why the army was sent
Many of the farmers were First World War veterans, settled on marginal land under [[soldier settlement|Soldier settlement in Australia]] schemes; farming at Campion had begun only in the early 1920s. When the Depression struck, the federal [[Grow More Wheat campaign]] of 1930 urged growers to plant more, on the promise of a guaranteed price that never arrived, while prices kept sliding. Then the emus came. After breeding, emus head from the inland toward the coast, and their [[migration|Emu migration]] now met cleared paddocks, ripening crops and farm dams. The birds ate and trampled the wheat and tore gaps in fences that let rabbits through.

A deputation of ex-soldiers asked the Minister for Defence, Sir [[George Pearce]], himself a Western Australian senator, for machine guns. He agreed on terms: soldiers would fire them, the state would pay for transport, and the farmers would supply food, lodging and the cost of ammunition. Some historians suggest the government also wanted to be seen helping, and to take the wind out of a growing [[push to leave the federation|1933 Western Australian secession referendum]]; to that end, a Fox Movietone newsreel cameraman was enlisted. One newspaper added that the gunners were to bring back 100 emu skins, so the feathers could become [[plumes for Light Horse hats|Emu plume]].
^ Western Australia's parliament passed its secession referendum act that same November; in April 1933, about two-thirds of voters chose to leave the federation.

## Machine guns in the paddocks
Rain delayed the start until early November, and from the first day the birds refused to be easy targets. Instead of bunching, the flocks split into small, fast-moving groups, and observers reported that each mob seemed to have a tall, black-plumed leader keeping watch. Meredith even mounted a gun on a truck, a makeshift [[technical|Technical (vehicle)]], but the truck could not gain on the birds and the ride was too rough for the gunner to fire. Wounded birds kept running. Meredith said the emus could "face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks", and the ornithologist Dominic Serventy later wrote that the "Emu command had evidently ordered guerrilla tactics."

10 Dec 1932 :: Meredith is recalled, claiming 986 emus for 9,860 rounds

## Bounties, fences and a punchline
Meredith's tally works out at exactly ten rounds per bird, and he claimed another 2,500 had died of their wounds; both figures have long been doubted. In parliament, Pearce was mocked as the minister of the Emu War, and when farmers asked for the army again in later years, they were turned down. What thinned the flocks was a [[bounty|Wildlife bounty]] paid on every emu killed. What held them back in the long run was fencing, above all the [[Rabbit-Proof Fence]], which ran right past Campion and found a second career as a barrier against migrating emus.
^ In six months of 1934, 57,034 emu bounties were claimed, nearly sixty times the army's claimed tally.

Emus remain common across mainland Australia. The war became a national joke, retold in a 2023 action comedy, while Campion, the township at its heart, was cancelled in 1972 and has all but vanished.
