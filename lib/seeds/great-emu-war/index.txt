In October 1932, about 20,000 emus, migrating inland after the breeding season, arrived in the wheatbelt around Campion and Walgoolan in Western Australia, where they flattened fences and trampled crops. The farmers who received them were mostly First World War veterans, settled on marginal land under the [[Soldier settlement scheme|soldier settlement scheme]], and they were being ruined by the collapse of wheat prices. Having served in the war, they knew exactly whom to ask for help: the army. The Minister for Defence, Sir George Pearce, approved a small force, and the "war" began on 2 November 1932 under Major G. P. W. Meredith of the [[Royal Australian Artillery]].

## The campaign

Meredith arrived with two soldiers, Sergeant S. McMurray and Gunner J. O'Halloran, plus two [[Lewis gun|Lewis guns]] and about 10,000 rounds. The first attempt, on 2 November, found a flock of roughly 50 birds, but they were out of range, and the emus broke into small groups that scattered rather than clumping into a target. An ambush at a dam produced a single burst before the gun jammed, and only a handful of birds fell. A truck-mounted gun, intended for pursuit, was useless: the vehicle could not keep pace with the birds on rough ground, and the gunner could barely hold his aim.

The mission was suspended on 8 November after press and parliamentary mockery, then resumed on 13 November, when the guns returned. Meredith reported that the birds seemed to post sentinels, and he is quoted as admiring the way an emu could carry several bullets and keep running. By his own account, the Lewis guns had killed perhaps 200 to 500 emus by early December. The numbers are uncertain because the official tally was never reliable, with estimates varying widely in the sources.

## Why the birds won

An [[Emu|emu]] stands up to about 1.9 metres tall, runs at over 50 km/h and, as Meredith found, has a body dense with feathers and muscle that absorbs small-calibre rounds. Crucially, emus are not herd animals in the way the soldiers had planned for; in a threat they disperse in all directions rather than bunching, which defeats an area weapon built to sweep a concentrated target. The Lewis gun, a .303-calibre air-cooled weapon from the First World War, was meant for infantry and aircraft, not for chasing erratic runners across scrubland.

The military was recalled for a reason that was not entirely military. Politicians in Canberra faced mockery, and an ornithologist, Dominic Serventy, later argued that the real damage came less from the guns than from the weather and farming itself. The emus themselves had a point: they were migrating toward water in a dry year, and the wheatfields, newly cleared and fenced, put crops directly across their ancient route.

## Aftermath

Farmers were not satisfied, and requests for more help persisted. A third operation in 1934 was reported to have killed hundreds, but the government instead leaned on a [[Bounty system for pest animals|bounty system]], which proved more effective: over 57,000 bounty claims were made in a six-month period in 1934. Fencing, such as the [[Rabbit-proof fence]] that already crossed Western Australia, remained the standard answer to wildlife damage, though no barrier has ever fully stopped emus.

The "war" has become an enduring joke, often retold as a military defeat by birds. It is better understood as a symptom of the [[Great Depression in Australia|Depression]], when desperate farmers and a government anxious for votes treated an ecological problem as an army problem. Much of its fame comes from a 1932 *Argus* and *Sydney Morning Herald* press, and from later retellings that sharpened the humour.
