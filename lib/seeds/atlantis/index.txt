Atlantis is a drowned empire that appears in exactly one place in ancient literature: two dialogues by [[Plato]], the [[Timaeus (dialogue)|Timaeus]] and the unfinished [[Critias (dialogue)|Critias]], written around 360 BC. In them, a great island power lying beyond the Pillars of Heracles launches an invasion of the Mediterranean, is checked by an ancient Athens, and then, in a single terrible day and night of earthquakes and floods, sinks beneath the sea. Every later telling, from Renaissance speculation to submarine-themed cartoons, descends from those few pages. Whether Plato was passing on a memory, bending a real disaster into a moral fable, or simply inventing the whole thing is the question that has kept the mystery alive for twenty-four centuries.

## Plato's story

The tale comes wrapped in layers of hearsay. In the dialogues, Critias recalls a story told to him as a boy by his grandfather, who had it from [[Solon]], the Athenian lawgiver, who in turn learned it from priests at Sais in Egypt around 600 BC. The priests supposedly read it from records kept in temple writing, and Solon, the account says, translated the names, which is where [[Hieroglyphs|hieroglyphic]] inscriptions enter the legend. They dated the war to about 9,000 years before Solon, which would put it near 9600 BC, long before any known city existed.

Atlantis was the realm of Poseidon, who fathered ten kings there with a mortal woman, Cleito. The capital was built around her hill in concentric rings, and the Critias gives its dimensions with unusual care: a central island 5 stadia across, ringed by alternating belts of sea and land measuring 1, 2 and 3 stadia in width. A [[Stadion (unit)|stadion]] was roughly 180 meters, so the whole city was about 5 kilometers wide. Bridges crossed the rings, a canal ran inland from the sea, and the walls were sheathed in [[Orichalcum|orichalcum]], tin and brass, with the temple of Poseidon at the center.

## A fable with a purpose

Plato's own habits are the strongest clue. The story is told to illustrate his ideal state, sketched in the [[Plato's Republic|Republic]]: ancient Athens is virtuous, disciplined and austere, while Atlantis is rich, expansive and finally corrupt. The empire's divine character fades as its rulers' mortal side grows, and it is punished. Plato elsewhere freely invented myths to make a philosophical point, and Aristotle, his pupil, is reported by later writers to have remarked that the man who invented Atlantis also destroyed it. Most classicists today read it as a constructed fable, although some argue Plato adapted real events.

The strangest feature is how thoroughly the story vanished after antiquity and how thoroughly it returned. In 1882, the American politician [[Ignatius Donnelly]] published *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World*, arguing that it was a real mid-ocean civilization, source of Egyptian and Maya culture. The notion that a sunken landmass lay beneath the Atlantic collapsed once the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]] was mapped and plate tectonics showed that the seafloor is young and no continent ever sank there.

## Candidates and what would settle it

Searchers have proposed nearly every drowned or devastated place. The leading scientific candidate is the Bronze Age explosion of Thera (Santorini), the [[Minoan eruption]] of roughly 1600 BC, one of the largest [[Volcanoes|volcanic]] events of the last few thousand years. It buried the town at Akrotiri and probably damaged Minoan Crete, a sea power with palaces and bull imagery that echo Plato's details. The weaknesses are scale and date: Plato's 9,000 years and his vast island do not fit, unless numbers were inflated by a factor of ten, an old and unprovable rescue.

Others point to [[Helike]], a Greek city swallowed by an earthquake and tsunami in 373 BC, within Plato's own lifetime, or to the flooding of [[Doggerland]], the North Sea plain submerged after the last ice age. Nothing would settle the matter except a source independent of Plato: an Egyptian text, or an archaeological site matching the ringed plan. None has appeared, and the burden rests on those who claim the story had a model beyond Plato's imagination.
