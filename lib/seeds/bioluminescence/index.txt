Bioluminescence is light made by living things. In most cases a small molecule called a luciferin reacts with oxygen in the grip of an enzyme called a luciferase, and the product is left so energised that it sheds the surplus as a photon. On land the glow belongs to fireflies, to fungi that make rotting wood shine as [[foxfire|Foxfire]], and to a scattering of worms, snails and millipedes. In the open ocean it is the rule: when researchers went through 17 years of video filmed between the surface and nearly 4,000 metres off California, about three quarters of the animals they saw could make light.
^ Among vertebrates, only fishes are known to make their own light; no glowing mammal, bird, reptile or amphibian has ever been found.

## One trick, many inventions
The words luciferin and luciferase were coined in the 1880s by the French scientist Raphaël Dubois, from the Latin for light-bearer, and they name jobs rather than particular chemicals. A firefly, a dinoflagellate and a glowing bacterium make light with unrelated molecules, a sign that the ability evolved separately many times: at least 94, by one 2021 count. Many sea creatures share one fuel, [[coelenterazine|Coelenterazine]], and some, including the jellyfish behind biology's famous green protein, cannot make it and must eat it.

Most living light in the sea is blue, the colour that travels farthest through seawater, and most deep-sea eyes are tuned to it. The [[stoplight loosejaw|Stoplight loosejaw]] exploits the gap: it shines a far-red beam that most animals around it cannot see, and detects the red with a pigment derived from chlorophyll, probably taken from its food.

## Hiding, warning, luring, courting
Often the light is camouflage. In the dim upper ocean, anything seen from below is a silhouette against the faint light from the surface, so many fish, shrimp and squid line their undersides with lights that match it, a trick called [[counter-illumination|Counter-illumination]]. The [[Hawaiian bobtail squid]] does it with bacteria it houses in a light organ, flushing most of them out each dawn. Those bacteria glow only when crowded, a habit that led to the discovery of [[quorum sensing|Quorum sensing]], the chemical headcount many bacteria use to act together.

Light can also be a cry for help. The [[burglar alarm hypothesis|Burglar alarm hypothesis]] holds that prey that lights up when attacked draws in its attacker's own predators. [[Edith Widder]] built an electronic lure that copies the alarm display of the deep-sea jellyfish Atolla, and in 2012 it helped record the first video of a giant squid in its natural habitat.
^ Giant squid eyes can reach about 27 centimetres across, perhaps to spot sperm whales by the glow they stir up as they swim.

Other glows are bait or courtship. In New Zealand's [[Waitomo Glowworm Caves]], fungus-gnat larvae shine from the ceiling and catch the insects they attract on hanging threads of sticky silk. Many fireflies flash species-specific codes, and [[Photuris]] females fake the replies of other species' females, then eat the males that come courting.

## Borrowed light, open questions
In the early 1960s [[Osamu Shimomura]] isolated two proteins from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria: aequorin, which flashes blue, and [[green fluorescent protein|Green fluorescent protein]], or GFP, which turns that blue into green. Fused to other proteins, GFP lets biologists watch them at work inside living cells, and the work won Shimomura a share of the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. One glowing mushroom makes its luciferin from caffeic acid, a compound plants also make, so a handful of fungal genes can light a plant: the [[Firefly Petunia]] has been on sale in the United States since 2024.

The largest displays are still poorly understood. [[Milky seas]], steady glows probably made by bacteria, can spread across 100,000 square kilometres of ocean. Satellites now track them, but a research ship has sampled one only once, by chance, in 1985.
