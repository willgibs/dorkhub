On a summer night, a [[firefly|Firefly (insect)]] lights its lantern with a soft glow that a person can follow across a meadow. Each species flashes in its own pattern, so the glow works as a code. In the deep sea, a tiny crustacean can release a cloud of blue light the moment it is disturbed. Both come from a chemical reaction, and that reaction produces light with almost no waste.

## Light without heat

The reaction is [[chemiluminescence|Chemiluminescence]]: a molecule called [[luciferin|Luciferin]] reacts with oxygen, helped by an enzyme, [[luciferase|Luciferase]], and the product gives off its energy as light. Luciferin is a family of molecules rather than one, which is why the glow of a firefly and the glow of a jellyfish are chemically different. Heat-driven light is the older route: an old filament bulb wastes more energy as warmth than as light, and [[fireworks|Fireworks]] glow because they burn hot. A jellyfish's blue flash is shifted to green by [[green fluorescent protein|Green fluorescent protein]], a second protein that absorbs the blue light and re-emits it.

## The deep ocean's lanterns

Deep water is dark, so light has become a common language there. A 2017 survey by Sönke Martini and Steven Haddock found that roughly three in four of the animals they sampled, from the surface down to the deep sea, could make light. The female [[anglerfish|Anglerfish]] dangles a glowing lure in front of her mouth, and in many species the glow comes from bacteria she carries inside it. Males of many of these species are far smaller and fuse to the female's body for life.

The [[Hawaiian bobtail squid|Hawaiian bobtail squid]] houses [[Vibrio fischeri|Vibrio fischeri]] bacteria in a light organ under its mantle. Each dawn it expels most of them, and each night the population grows back from the seawater around it. Many deep-sea fish use light organs along their bellies in a similar way, a tactic known as [[counter-illumination|Counter-illumination]].

::: theories
Counter-illumination :: a glowing underside hides a silhouette from predators below :: accepted
Prey lure :: light attracts prey close enough to catch :: accepted
Startle :: a sudden flash confuses or scares off a predator :: partly
Detox :: light is a side effect of clearing harmful oxygen compounds :: disputed
:::

## Sea sparkle and bright bays

Along many coasts, the most familiar sea light is sea sparkle, made by [[dinoflagellates|Dinoflagellate]], single-celled plankton that flash when a wave or a swimmer's hand disturbs them. In [[Mosquito Bay]] on the island of Vieques, Puerto Rico, a dense population makes the water one of the brightest bioluminescent bays on Earth. The glow is strongest on moonless nights, when nothing outshines it. Because the light comes from living cells, it can vary from night to night with the plankton's numbers and the water's movement.

^ Most of the light in a glowing wave comes from single-celled plankton, each cell far smaller than a grain of sand.
