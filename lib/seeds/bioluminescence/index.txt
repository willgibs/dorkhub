In the open ocean, a ship's wake can turn to blue fire. Each ripple jostles single-celled [[dinoflagellate|Dinoflagellate]]s, and each cell answers with a flash lasting a fraction of a second. Out of sight below, something far larger is going on: below roughly 200 meters, where sunlight fades into permanent dusk and then total black, most animals living in the water column make their own light. Trawl nets hauled up from the [[Mesopelagic zone]] bring up fish, squid, shrimp and jellies whose bodies are studded with glowing organs, and surveys of the deep ocean suggest that the great majority of the individuals swimming there are luminous. Bioluminescence is not a curiosity of the deep; it is the dominant visual language of the largest habitat on Earth.

## The chemistry of cold light

The basic reaction is simple. A small molecule, generically called [[luciferin|Luciferin]], is oxidized, and the energy released leaves the product in an excited state that sheds the surplus as a photon. An enzyme, [[luciferase|Luciferase]], usually speeds the reaction; in some animals the machinery is a photoprotein that stores the reaction ready to fire when triggered by calcium. "Luciferin" is a job description, not one compound: the fireflies, the bacteria, the dinoflagellates and the marine coelenterazine users each use chemically unrelated molecules. Evolution has invented light-making independently at least 40 times, by common estimates, which is why glowing species turn up in bacteria, fungi, insects, fish and worms but never in birds or mammals.

The light is called cold because almost none of the energy is wasted as heat, unlike an incandescent bulb, which loses most of its input that way. The reaction is a form of [[chemiluminescence]], and it is why fireworks make a telling contrast: a firework burns metal salts at high temperature and gets color from heat, while a glowing jelly gets color from a reaction at the temperature of seawater.

^ Most deep-sea light is blue-green because that wavelength travels farthest through seawater; many deep fish can see it, and almost nothing else.

## What the light is for

The uses are blunt and varied. The [[anglerfish]] dangles a luminous lure, glowing through symbiotic bacteria, in front of its mouth. Hatchetfish and the [[Hawaiian bobtail squid]] practice [[counterillumination]]: they match the faint light coming down from the surface with glowing organs on their undersides, erasing their silhouettes from predators looking up. The [[vampire squid]] releases a cloud of glowing mucus instead of ink, since a cloud of dark would be invisible down there. Others flash as alarms, as burglar alarms that attract a bigger predator to whatever is attacking them, or, on land, as courtship: each [[firefly]] species flashes a distinct pattern, and some predatory females mimic the signals of other species to lure in males.

## Glowing from the sea to the lab

Light can be seen at scale. Mariners have long described a "[[milky sea]]," a glow stretching across tens of thousands of square kilometers of the Indian Ocean; satellites have recorded such events, and the glow is thought to come from luminous bacteria. On land, rotting wood sometimes glows green with [[foxfire]], produced by fungi. And in 1962 Osamu Shimomura isolated a glowing protein from the jellyfish [[Aequorea victoria]]; its companion [[green fluorescent protein]] became one of biology's most useful tags, letting scientists watch genes switch on in living cells. That work earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.

Bioluminescence also leaves puzzles. How often the light is a defense, a lure or only a metabolic byproduct is still debated for many species, and the deep sea is so poorly sampled that new luminous species are routinely described.
