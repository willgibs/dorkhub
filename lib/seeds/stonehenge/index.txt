Stonehenge is the best-known prehistoric monument in Europe, a ring of standing stones on the chalk grassland of Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England. What visitors see today is the last arrangement of a site that was reworked for roughly 1,500 years. It began around 3000 BC as a circular ditch and bank, with a ring of pits holding timber or small stones, and about 2500 BC it received its great stones: an outer circle of tall [[sarsen]] uprights carrying a continuous ring of horizontal lintels, with a horseshoe of even bigger sarsen trilithons inside. Among them stood smaller [[bluestone]]s, brought from far away. The largest sarsens weigh around 25 tonnes and the tallest upright rises about 7 metres.

^ The lintels are held on by [[mortise and tenon]] joints and fitted to each other by tongue-and-groove, carpentry techniques borrowed from woodworking and applied to stone.

## Two kinds of stone

The sarsens are a hard silicified sandstone, and geochemical work published in 2020 by a team led by David Nash traced most of them to West Woods, about 25 km north of the monument, near Marlborough. The bluestones are a different story. Many are dolerite and rhyolite that came from the [[Preseli Hills]] of southwest Wales, roughly 240 km away, where quarries at Carn Goedog and Craig Rhos-y-felin show signs of prehistoric extraction. How the stones, up to about 2 tonnes each, travelled that distance is still argued, though the old idea that glaciers delivered them is now widely rejected by specialists in favour of human transport.

The most surprising passenger is the [[Altar Stone]], a 6-tonne slab of sandstone lying at the centre of the monument. For a century it was assumed to be Welsh. In 2024 a team from Curtin University and University College London matched mineral grains in it to rocks of the [[Orcadian Basin]] in northeast Scotland, about 750 km away. That makes it the longest known movement of a megalith in Britain, and it implies that the monument's builders were connected to communities across the entire island.

::: timeline
3000 BC :: Circular ditch and bank dug; pits ring the interior
2500 BC :: Sarsen circle, trilithons and bluestones set up
2400 BC :: Beaker-using people begin arriving in Britain
2300 BC :: Bluestones rearranged into circle and horseshoe
1500 BC :: Last major activity at the site
:::

## Who built it, and why

The builders left no writing, but their settlements survive. At [[Durrington Walls]], 3 km northeast, excavations led by [[Mike Parker Pearson]] uncovered a huge village of houses from about 2500 BC, with pig bones showing midwinter and midsummer feasts. A ceremonial [[Avenue (Stonehenge)]] links the stones to the River Avon, and the alignment of the monument matches the sunrise at the summer solstice and sunset at the [[winter solstice]], which suggests a calendrical meaning, though not necessarily an astronomical observatory in the modern sense. Cremated remains of about 60 people buried in the early phase show it was also a cemetery, which dating by [[radiocarbon dating]] places mostly between 3000 and 2500 BC.

Ancient [[DNA]] has changed the picture of the people themselves. A 2019 study in Nature Ecology & Evolution, led by Selina Brace and Ian Barnes, showed that Neolithic farmers in Britain descended mostly from Anatolian farmers who moved west through Iberia, not from the earlier hunter-gatherers. A few centuries after the sarsens went up, migrants linked to the [[Beaker culture]] arrived from the continent and replaced most of Britain's gene pool within a few generations. One of them, the [[Amesbury Archer]], was buried 5 km from the stones around 2300 BC with gold ornaments and was raised in the Alps region, a reminder that travellers reached the site from far beyond Britain.

^ The Beaker newcomers who replaced most British ancestry arrived centuries after the stones were raised, so they inherited Stonehenge rather than built it.
