A figure skater glides over a sheet of ice on a blade a few millimetres wide, and a pebble-sized curling stone slides some forty metres down a sheet of ice that has been deliberately sprayed with droplets to make it rougher. Ice is among the slipperiest substances we meet, with a coefficient of [[Friction]] that can fall below 0.01 near the melting point, lower than a greased steel surface. Yet the question of why has been argued over for more than 150 years, and the answer you were probably taught at school, that a skater's weight melts the ice beneath the blade, does not survive a calculation.

^ Ice that is slippery at -1 °C can be grippy at -30 °C: the same skates that glide in a warm rink can feel like they are dragging through sand in deep Arctic cold.

## The pressure story, and why it fails

The idea of [[Pressure melting]] goes back to the 1840s, when the Thomson brothers, James and William (later Lord Kelvin), noticed that ice, unusually, is less dense than water, so squeezing it should lower its melting point. The effect is real but tiny: about 0.0075 °C for every atmosphere of added pressure. A 70-kilogram skater standing on a blade with a contact area of a few square millimetres might exert perhaps 100 atmospheres, which depresses the melting point by under one degree. At -10 °C, a common rink or winter temperature, you would need roughly 1,300 atmospheres, far beyond anything a skate produces, and people skate happily at -20 °C.

The same idea was once used to explain how a weighted wire cuts through a block of ice and leaves it whole behind, a trick called [[Regelation]]. Later work showed that heat conduction through the wire plays a major role, so even that classic demonstration is not clean evidence for pressure melting.

## Heat and a skin of water

A second suggestion is [[Frictional heating]]. In the 1930s Frank Philip Bowden and T. P. Hughes at Cambridge measured ice friction and argued that sliding warms the surface until it melts, lubricating the contact. This clearly operates at high speed, where a skate or sled generates real heat, and it explains why friction rises with speed on some surfaces. But it cannot explain why ice is slippery to a stationary foot on a cold morning, before anything has moved.

That puzzle points to the oldest clue. In 1850 [[Michael Faraday]] pressed two blocks of ice together and found they froze to each other, which he took as evidence that the surface was covered in a thin film of liquid even well below freezing. Modern experiments have confirmed that something like this exists, the [[Quasi-liquid layer]], a disordered skin whose [[Hydrogen bond]] network is looser than the crystal beneath. The phenomenon of [[Premelting]] means surfaces of many crystals begin to disorder before the bulk melts.

::: figures
0.0075 °C :: melting-point drop per atmosphere of pressure
~1,300 atm :: pressure needed to melt ice at -10 °C
0.01 :: approximate friction coefficient of ice near 0 °C
:::

## What the surface really looks like

Instruments built to see this layer disagree about its thickness. Techniques such as [[Atomic force microscopy]], X-ray scattering and [[Sum-frequency generation spectroscopy]] suggest the disordered layer is only a nanometre or so thick at -10 °C, growing to tens of nanometres near 0 °C. Whether it is truly liquid, or merely a loosely bonded layer of molecules different from the bulk [[Ice Ih]] crystal, is part of the dispute.

In 2018 a team led by Mischa Bonn and Ellen Backus at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research, working with Daniel Bonn in Amsterdam, proposed a unifying picture. Their simulations and experiments suggest that the water molecules at the surface are pulled and rotated by the sliding material, so that dipoles from the slider disrupt the crystal; this would explain slipperiness without needing a pre-existing liquid layer or heat. Other groups still defend the role of the premelted layer, and the practical side is mostly managed empirically: [[Curling]] icemakers pebble the sheet and skate-sharpeners hollow-grind blades, tuning ice by feel while the physics remains contested.

Bigger scales matter too. Every [[Glacier]] slides on a basal water film, and the study of ice's strange surface feeds into everything from tire design to avalanche prediction.
