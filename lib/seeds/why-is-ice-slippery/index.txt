Few things slide as easily as a blade on ice: a speed skate meets a friction coefficient of about 0.005, far below Teflon on steel. The leading explanation is that ice wears a thin, disordered, water-like skin even below freezing, and that sliding adds heat that thickens it. People glided on [[bone skates|Bone skates]] for thousands of years before the first scientific explanation in the 1880s, and the answer has kept shifting since.

::: theories
Pressure melting :: A skater's weight melts the ice under the blade into water :: rejected
Frictional melting :: Heat from sliding melts a thin film of water to glide on :: partly
Premelted skin :: A liquid-like surface layer, there even below freezing, lubricates the ice :: partly
Cold amorphization :: Sliding contact scrambles the surface into a viscous, liquid-like layer :: disputed
:::

## The weight of the skater

[[Ordinary ice|Ice Ih]] takes up about 9 percent more room than the water it froze from, so squeezing it lowers its melting point. James Thomson predicted this in 1849, and his brother William, later Lord Kelvin, confirmed it in 1850. In 1886 the Irish physicist [[John Joly]] reasoned that a skate blade touches so little ice that it presses at about 466 atmospheres, enough to melt ice down to roughly −3.5 °C.

The effect is simply too small. The [[Clausius–Clapeyron relation]] puts it at about 0.0075 °C per atmosphere, yet people skate and ski at −30 °C, and a walker in flat-soled boots still slips. Pressure matters, if at all, only within a few degrees of melting.

^ Below about −22 °C, squeezing cannot melt ordinary ice at all: enough pressure turns it into a denser crystal, such as ice III, instead.

## The heat of sliding

In the late 1930s Frank Bowden and T. P. Hughes took a rival idea, that friction's own heat melts a film of water, to an ice cave at the [[Jungfraujoch]], high in the Swiss Alps. If they were right, a slider that drains heat away should grip harder, and metal sliders did drag more than poor conductors.

Slow sliders make little heat, and Ming engineers seem to have worked around that. A 1557 record describes a stone of about 112 tonnes dragged by sledge along an [[ice road|Forbidden City ice roads]] to Beijing's Forbidden City. Wetting the ice, a 2013 study calculated, would have let fewer than 50 men haul a load that needed at least 330 on bare ice.

The film is odd, too. In 2019 a Paris team found it only 100 to 500 nanometres thick under a gliding bead, and up to a hundred times more viscous than water. A water-repelling coating cut the friction as much as tenfold, which may help explain why [[ski wax|Ski wax]] works.

## The unfrozen skin

None of this explains why ice is treacherous to someone standing still. In a Royal Institution lecture in June 1850, [[Michael Faraday]] proposed that ice carries a thin film of water on its surface. His evidence was [[regelation|Regelation]], the way two pieces of ice fuse on touching, which he read as the film freezing once trapped between them. James Thomson blamed pressure instead, and his view prevailed for nearly a century.

Physicists now call it a [[premelted|Premelting]] layer. Surface molecules lack neighbours above them, so they are held less tightly, and X-rays, nuclear magnetic resonance and atomic force microscopes have all detected the disorder. Measurements disagree on its thickness, but it grows as ice warms toward 0 °C.

^ Metals do it too: in 1985 physicists found a lead crystal's surface disordered at 307 °C, twenty degrees below lead's 327 °C melting point.

In 2018 researchers in Amsterdam and Mainz found that some surface molecules, held by two hydrogen bonds instead of three, roll freely across the ice. Their steel slid best near −7 °C, the temperature speed-skating rinks favour; warmer ice turns soft, and a slider digs in.

The argument is not over. Simulations from Saarland University, published in 2025, suggest that a slider's contact and motion scramble the outer layers into an [[amorphous|Amorphous ice]], liquid-like film without melting them, even near absolute zero. In deep cold, though, that film is far more viscous than honey. Polar explorers knew cold snow's drag well: [[The Worst Journey in the World]], Apsley Cherry-Garrard's account of Scott's last expedition, likens sledging in extreme cold to hauling over sand.
