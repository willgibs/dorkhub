A coral reef is a rampart of limestone raised by animals, and a city grown on top of it. Wade over the edge of one in the tropics and the water seems to boil with life: fish in every colour, crabs wedged in crevices, sea fans, giant clams, sharks idling in the shade of branching thickets. The reefs of the world together cover less than 1% of the sea floor, yet they are home to an estimated quarter of all marine species, a density of life that rivals rainforest and far exceeds the surrounding open ocean. Most of the architects are polyps, soft-bodied relatives of jellyfish a few millimetres across, each drawing [[calcium carbonate|Calcium carbonate]] from seawater to build a cup-shaped skeleton beneath itself. Over thousands of years, trillions of those cups pile into structures visible from orbit.

^ Reef-building corals live only where water stays above roughly 18°C, which is why reefs hug the tropics and why a degree or two of warming matters so much.

## A partnership in clear water

Reefs thrive in water that is almost empty of nutrients, which looks like a paradox. The explanation is a bargain: inside the tissues of reef-building corals live microscopic algae called [[zooxanthellae]], which photosynthesise and hand much of their sugar to the host, sometimes supplying the large majority of its energy. The coral in turn provides shelter and the nitrogen waste the algae need. This tight recycling is why reefs flourish in clear, sunlit, nutrient-poor shallows, and why most reef-building species are confined to the top few dozen metres where light penetrates.

The partnership is also the reef's weak point. When water runs too hot, the algae are expelled, the coral turns ghostly white, and unless temperatures fall within weeks the animal starves. This is [[coral bleaching]], and mass events have struck the [[Great Barrier Reef]] repeatedly, with severe episodes in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020 and 2022.

## Slow construction, sudden change

Reefs grow by only millimetres to a few centimetres a year, so large ones are old. The living veneer of the Great Barrier Reef sits on foundations laid down over hundreds of thousands of years, but the surface we see is young: during the last [[ice ages|Ice ages]], sea level stood about 120 metres lower, and today's shallow reef platforms were dry land. As the ice melted, seas rose and corals climbed upward with them, so most living reef dates only from the last 8,000 years or so. In 1842 Charles Darwin used this logic to explain the [[atoll]], a ring of reef around an empty lagoon: a volcanic island slowly sinks while the coral keeps growing upward, leaving only the circle. Drilling at Eniwetok in the 1950s found volcanic rock beneath more than a kilometre of coral limestone, confirming him.

^ Reefs can pull off a similar trick at depth: [[mesophotic reefs|Mesophotic reef]], found 30 to 150 metres down, survive in dim light that shallow species could not use.

## Who lives there, and who eats whom

A reef is crowded because space is the scarce resource and every niche gets filled. [[Parrotfish]] scrape algae off the rock with beak-like teeth and excrete much of it as fine white sand, which is where many tropical beaches come from. Cleaner fish such as the [[cleaner wrasse]] run stations where much larger predators queue to have parasites picked from their skin and even their mouths. [[Octopuses]] hunt among the crevices, and in some cases cooperate with groupers and other fish, which flush prey from hiding. A single night each year can look like a snowstorm in reverse: during [[coral spawning]] off the Great Barrier Reef, usually after a late-spring full moon, colonies release bundles of eggs and sperm in near synchrony.

Reefs have their own enemies. Outbreaks of the [[crown-of-thorns starfish]] can strip coral over huge areas, and the absorption of carbon dioxide by seawater, [[ocean acidification]], makes it harder for corals to lay down skeleton at all. Even so, reefs have recovered from past hammerings, which is part of why researchers still argue about how much can be saved, and how fast the damage can be slowed.
