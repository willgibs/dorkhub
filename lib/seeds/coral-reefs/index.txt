From a plane above north-eastern Australia, the [[Great Barrier Reef]] looks like a mosaic of blue-green patches laid across the sea. Look closer and the mosaic turns out to be built by animals, tiny polyps that have been stacking limestone skeletons for thousands of years. Most of the reef's bulk is old skeleton, and only a thin living skin of coral covers it at any moment.

## Animals that run partly on sunlight

Each [[Coral (animal)|coral]] is a colony of polyps, soft-bodied animals that sit inside cups of calcium carbonate they draw from seawater. Their tissues host single-celled algae called [[Zooxanthellae]], which photosynthesise and pass sugars to the coral, while the coral gives the algae shelter and nutrients. That partnership is why reef-building corals need clear, sunlit water, and why most reefs stop growing where light runs thin.

Reefs cover very little of the planet. Coral reefs occupy less than 1 per cent of the ocean floor, yet they are home to an estimated quarter of all marine species. That makes them one of the densest concentrations of life in the sea, packed into a sliver of the sea floor.
^ Many of today's reefs grow on the skeletons of older reefs, so the living layer sits on a foundation far older than itself.

## Sea level and sinking islands

In 1842 [[Charles Darwin]] published his account of coral reefs, written after the voyage of HMS Beagle, and his explanation of atolls has shaped the field since. His idea ran from a [[Fringing reef|fringing reef]] that hugs a shore, to a barrier reef set further out, to an [[Atoll|atoll]]. It also predicted what drilling would find beneath an atoll, so the idea could be tested rather than merely argued. In the early 1950s, drilling at [[Eniwetok Atoll]] reached volcanic rock beneath a thick column of reef limestone, which fitted the prediction. The test did not settle everything, because sea-level swings during the [[Ice ages|ice ages]] also left their mark on reef growth.
::: theories
Subsidence :: Atolls form as volcanic islands sink while corals grow upward. :: accepted
Glacial control :: Sea-level swings in the ice ages shaped reef growth on shelves. :: partly
:::

At the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], roughly 20,000 years ago, global sea level stood about 120 metres lower than it does now. Shelves that are now a few dozen metres under the sea were then dry or close to it, so reefs that had once grown there were left exposed. When the ice melted and the sea climbed again, many of the reefs on today's shelves grew upward through that rise, and some drowned where the water rose faster than they could keep up.

## Too warm, too acidic

The reef's fragility shows most clearly in [[Coral bleaching|bleaching]]. When water stays too warm for too long, corals expel their algae, lose their colour and turn white, and without the sugars the algae supply, they can starve unless the algae return. The [[Great Barrier Reef]] suffered mass bleaching in 1998, and again in 2016 and 2017, as heat waves swept along its length.
^ Bleached coral is not automatically dead, and many colonies recover if cooler water returns in time.

A second threat works more slowly: as the ocean absorbs carbon dioxide, its chemistry shifts toward [[Ocean acidification|acidification]], which makes it harder for corals and other organisms to build their skeletons. Outbreaks of the [[Crown-of-thorns starfish]] add a biological pressure, since the starfish feed on coral tissue, and outbreaks can strip large stretches of reef faster than corals regrow. Where pollution and warm water arrive together, recovery is slower, which is why managers focus on the pressures they can reduce locally.
