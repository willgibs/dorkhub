Take a square of paper, fold it with no scissors and no glue, and it can become a crane with a head, two wings and a tail. The Japanese word for the craft, origami, joins oru, to fold, with kami, paper, which becomes gami in the compound. Folders often choose [[washi|Washi]], a handmade Japanese paper whose long fibres hold a crease. Every fold decides where the paper will bend, and a few creases can turn a flat square into a three-dimensional animal.
^ Kami, the word for paper, is written with a different character from kami, the word for a god or spirit.

## From ritual paper to the thousand cranes

Paper reached Japan from China, and folded paper there grew up around ceremony, wrapping gifts and marking offerings. The [[paper cranes|Paper crane]] became the craft's emblem because they stood for long life, and a legend holds that folding a thousand of them grants a wish. In 1797 a Japanese book called [[Senbazuru Orikata]] published instructions for a chain of a thousand cranes, the senbazuru that gives it its name.

[[Sadako Sasaki]] was two when the atomic bomb fell on Hiroshima in 1945, and in 1955 she was diagnosed with leukemia, folding cranes in hospital in the hope of recovery. She died that year at twelve, and her classmates folded more cranes in her memory. The [[Children's Peace Monument]] in Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Park, unveiled in 1958, shows her holding a golden crane aloft.

## A fold that opened a satellite

Koryo Miura, a Japanese space scientist, devised the [[Miura fold]] for the solar arrays of spacecraft. The Space Flyer Unit, a Japanese satellite launched in 1995, carried an array folded this way and unfolded it in orbit. Engineers call structures that pack small and then open on command [[deployable|Deployable structure]], and the Miura fold is a standard example.
::: steps
Crease :: a flat sheet is marked with a grid of mountain and valley creases
Collapse :: the grid folds flat, each parallelogram stacking onto the next
Pull :: one pull on the sheet opens the zigzag back to full size
:::

[[Kirigami]], the cut-and-fold cousin of origami, makes a different trade. Traditional origami allows no cuts, but slits let a folded sheet stretch in one direction while the folds keep it compact. The price is strength, because every cut is a place where the sheet can tear.

## Rules for where the paper bends

Most complex models begin as a [[crease pattern|Crease pattern]], a map of their folds, each marked as a mountain or a valley. Only some patterns fold flat, and at every vertex that does, the mountain and valley folds differ by exactly two. That rule is [[Maekawa's theorem]], named after the Japanese origami researcher Jun Maekawa, and it can be checked before anything is folded.

[[Akira Yoshizawa]] popularised wet folding, which shapes damp paper into soft curves that dry creases cannot hold. He also worked out, with the American folder Samuel Randlett, a diagram system that is still used to publish models. Diagrams let a model travel from one folder to another without a demonstration.

## Folding DNA

The craft reached the molecular scale in 2006, when Paul Rothemund at Caltech published a way to fold [[DNA]] into flat shapes such as a smiley face and a star. He began with one DNA strand, and short pieces called staples pinned it into a planned form. The method came to be called DNA origami, and it showed that a designed sequence can dictate a shape.
^ The paper was a virus's genome, from a virus that infects bacteria, not a sheet of cellulose.
