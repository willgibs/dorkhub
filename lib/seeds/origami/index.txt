Origami is the art of making shapes by folding a single sheet, and its best-known product is the [[Orizuru|paper crane]]: a square becomes a bird in about twenty creases, with no glue and, by the strictest tradition, no scissors. Paper arrived in Japan from China around the sixth to seventh century, and folding was at first ceremonial, wrapping gifts and sacred objects. The first surviving instructions for a recreational model, a crane, appear in the 1797 book *Senbazuru orikata*, "How to fold one thousand cranes". The word "origami" itself, from *ori* (fold) and *kami* (paper), only became standard in the twentieth century, displacing older terms such as *orikata*.

The modern art owes much to [[Akira Yoshizawa]] (1911 to 2005), a Tokyo factory worker turned full-time folder who invented a notation of dotted lines and arrows still used in nearly every diagram today. He also pioneered wet-folding, dampening heavy paper so it can be molded into curves with the look of sculpture. Through exhibitions in Amsterdam in 1955 and later in the United States, he carried the craft far beyond Japan.

^ In Japanese tradition, folding a thousand cranes is said to earn a wish; Sadako Sasaki, a child of the Hiroshima bombing, began folding them in 1955, making the crane a symbol of peace.

## The geometry underneath

Folding looks like play, but it is a rigorous system of construction. In 1989 Humiaki Huzita laid out six basic single-fold operations, and Koshiro Hatori later noted a seventh; together the [[Huzita–Hatori axioms]] describe every way of making one crease by aligning existing points and lines. Their power exceeds compass and straightedge: a single fold can solve a cubic equation, which means origami can perform the [[Angle trisection|trisection of an angle]] and double the cube, two classical problems proved impossible with Euclid's tools. Whether a given crease pattern can actually be pressed flat is its own hard question, now called [[Flat-foldability]]; Marshall Bern and Barry Hayes proved in 1996 that deciding it in general is NP-hard.

The broadest result is that, in principle, a sufficiently large sheet can be folded into any shape with flat sides, with no cuts at all. Erik Demaine and colleagues proved this in 1999 with a "fold and cut" theorem: any straight-line figure can be cut out of a folded sheet with one scissor stroke. It is one reason [[Kirigami]], the variant that permits cutting, is mathematically a close cousin rather than a separate art.

::: steps
Choose :: Pick a target shape, such as an insect with six legs, two antennae and wings.
Flaps :: Represent each appendage as a circle on the square; circles must not overlap.
Pack :: Arrange the circles so the paper is used efficiently.
Creases :: Software computes the crease pattern connecting the circles.
Fold :: The folder collapses the pattern into a flat base, then shapes it.
:::

## From paper to engineering

[[Robert J. Lang]], a laser physicist who became a full-time folder in 2001, turned this circle-packing idea into a program, [[TreeMaker]], that designs a crease pattern for any stick-figure animal. The same optimization lets engineers think about packing large surfaces into small volumes. The most famous example is the [[Miura fold]], devised by astrophysicist Koryo Miura in the 1970s: a grid of parallelograms where adjacent panels fold along a zigzag, so that pulling on two opposite corners opens the whole sheet at once and pushing them together collapses it. Because every crease moves in concert, there is no need to unfold fold by fold, which makes it ideal for solar arrays. It flew on the Japanese [[Space Flyer Unit]] in 1995, when a pair of solar panels was deployed from orbit.

Folding principles now turn up in car airbags, whose creases are simulated to inflate evenly, in the [[Heart stent|self-expanding stents]] that are collapsed to pass through a narrow artery and then open, and in map and packaging design. Miura's pattern also appears on maps that open with a single pull.

^ The Miura fold was independently noticed in nature: the crumpled leaves of hornbeam and beech trees unfold from their buds along a very similar pattern.

At the smallest scale, the idea goes molecular. In 2006 Paul Rothemund at Caltech published [[DNA origami]], using a long strand of viral [[DNA]] guided by about two hundred short "staple" strands to fold into smiley faces and maps a hundred nanometres across. Researchers have since built nano-boxes with lids that open on a molecular key, aiming at targeted drug delivery. It is the same logic as a crane from a square, with the paper replaced by a molecule.
