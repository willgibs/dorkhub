Origami is the art of making forms from a single sheet of paper by folding alone. Its most famous product is the paper crane, but the craft reaches from a child's boat to models with hundreds of folded points, and from the shelf of a gift shop to the deployment mechanism of a satellite. Paper itself reached Japan from China in the early seventh century, and folded paper has been used there ever since in ceremony: wrapping gifts, and the paper butterflies placed on sake bottles at weddings. The oldest known printed instructions for a folded figure are in Japanese books of the 1700s, including a 1797 work on folding a thousand cranes, though how much of the tradition is older than its surviving documents is hard to say.

The modern art was largely made by one man. [[Akira Yoshizawa]] (1911 to 2005) left factory work in Tokyo to fold full-time, and in the 1950s he devised a notation of dashed lines, arrows and symbols that is still the standard language of diagrams worldwide. He also pioneered [[Wet-folding|wet-folding]], dampening heavy paper so it can be shaped into smooth, rounded animals with a sculptural look. Exhibitions in Amsterdam in 1955 and later in the United States carried his work to the West, where it influenced a generation of folders.

^ The thousand-crane tradition gained a modern weight through [[Sadako Sasaki]], a Hiroshima survivor who folded cranes in hospital in 1955 and became a symbol of peace.

## When folding became mathematics

From the 1980s, folders and mathematicians began asking what the craft could in principle do. A few clean rules emerged. [[Kawasaki's theorem]] says that at any vertex of a flat-folded crease pattern, alternate angles add to 180 degrees, and a related result says the number of mountain and valley creases differs by exactly two. [[Flat-foldability]] in general turned out to be computationally hard: in 1996 Marshall Bern and Barry Hayes proved that deciding whether an arbitrary crease pattern folds flat is NP-hard.

The [[Huzita–Hatori axioms]], formalized by Humiaki Huzita and Koshiro Hatori, list seven basic ways to place a single fold relative to existing points and lines. Their power is striking: straightedge-and-compass geometry cannot trisect an angle or double a cube, but origami folds can, since one of the axioms solves cubic equations. [[Robert J. Lang]], a laser physicist turned full-time folder, turned the theory into software called TreeMaker, which computes a crease pattern for any stick-figure "tree" of limbs, so that a beetle with legs, antennae and horns can be designed on a computer.

## Folds that leave the table

The best-known engineering fold is the [[Miura fold]], devised by the Japanese astrophysicist Koryo Miura in the 1970s. Its crease pattern is a grid of parallelograms with zigzag lines, so that pulling opposite corners opens or closes the entire sheet in one motion, with no creases fighting each other and every panel stressed evenly. A version of it flew on the Japanese [[Space Flyer Unit]], launched in 1995, to unfurl a solar array; the appeal of the pattern is that a single actuator can deploy the whole panel.

Elsewhere the same ideas fold things smaller than a fingernail. Origami-derived designs shape [[Stent|stents]] that are squeezed through a blood vessel and then expand, and the way an [[Airbag]] packs flat owes to folding simulations that were first built for origami. Cut-and-fold hybrids belong to [[Kirigami]], and repeating pleat patterns known as [[Tessellation (origami)|origami tessellations]] give structure to materials that stiffen or bend in chosen directions. At the molecular scale, a single long strand of [[DNA]] can be folded by short "staple" strands into smiley faces and tiny boxes, a method Paul Rothemund published in 2006 and named DNA origami.

^ Rothemund's 2006 DNA smiley faces were about 100 nanometres across, roughly a thousand times narrower than a human hair.
