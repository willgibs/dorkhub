Run a fingertip across a pane of glass and you leave behind a faint record: a map of ridges, thinner than a hair's width, laid down in a pattern that no one else alive shares. Fingerprints are the impressions left by the friction ridges on the skin of fingers, palms and soles, and they are among the most familiar and most argued-over objects in forensic science. They begin forming in the womb, around the tenth week of pregnancy, and by about the twenty-fourth week the pattern is fixed for life. Cuts and burns heal into the same design, because the blueprint lives in the deeper layer of skin, not on the surface.

The pattern is not simply inherited. [[Monozygotic twins|Identical twins]] share their [[DNA]] almost entirely, yet their prints differ, because the ridges are shaped by chance conditions in the womb: the pressure of amniotic fluid, the position of the hand, the thickness of the growing finger pad. Even the ten fingers on one person differ from each other. Prints do run in families in broad strokes, since the general pattern type (loop, whorl or arch) is partly heritable, which is why the study of ridges, [[Dermatoglyphics]], has long been used as a window into development.

^ Koalas have fingerprints strikingly like ours, nearly indistinguishable under a microscope, even though primates and marsupials split from a common ancestor over 100 million years ago. Evolution seems to have invented them twice.

## From clay seals to a crime scene

Ancient Babylonian and Chinese records show fingers pressed into clay, and in 1858 the British administrator William Herschel in India began taking handprints on contracts to stop signatories disowning them. The scientific leap came in 1880, when the Scottish physician [[Henry Faulds]], working in Tokyo, published a note in Nature arguing that prints could identify criminals. Faulds even helped clear an innocent man in a Tokyo theft case by showing the sooty prints at the scene did not match him. [[Francis Galton]] then supplied the statistics, publishing his 1892 book Finger Prints and estimating the odds of two matching at about one in 64 billion.

Before prints, police leaned on the [[Bertillon system]], which identified people by body measurements. The [[Will West case]] of 1903 at Leavenworth, a story often repeated though its details are embroidered, is said to have shown that two men could share near-identical measurements while their prints differed. Meanwhile [[Juan Vucetich]], in Argentina, secured the first murder conviction by fingerprint in 1892, when a bloody thumbprint exposed a mother who had blamed another for killing her children.

## How a match is made, and where it fails

Examiners classify ridge details called minutiae: forks, endings, islands and dots. In a clean rolled print taken in ink or by scanner, these are easy to read, and databases like the FBI's [[Integrated Automated Fingerprint Identification System]] can search tens of millions of records in minutes. Crime scenes are harder. A [[Latent fingerprint development|latent print]] is an invisible smear of sweat and oil that must be revealed with powder, superglue fumes or chemical dyes, and it is usually partial, smudged or overlaid by other marks.

Here the claim of infallibility cracks. In 2004, after the Madrid train bombings, the FBI declared a latent print matched Oregon lawyer Brandon Mayfield; Spanish police later linked it to an Algerian man, and Mayfield, held for two weeks, was cleared in the [[Mayfield affair]]. Studies of examiners shown the same prints months apart have sometimes produced inconsistent verdicts, and unlike many countries, the United States has no fixed number of matching points required.

The ridges themselves have a job. They improve grip, and the [[Meissner's corpuscle|Meissner's corpuscles]] nestled beneath them sharpen touch, though a 2009 study found fingerprints may actually wick moisture in ways that matter for grip, and the exact purpose remains debated. A rare genetic condition, [[Adermatoglyphia]], leaves people with smooth fingertips entirely, which can complicate border crossings.
