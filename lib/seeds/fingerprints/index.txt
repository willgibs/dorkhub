Run a finger across a sheet of paper and you are using an instrument that took about fourteen weeks to build. Each fingertip carries a field of raised ridges, typically a few tenths of a millimetre high and spaced roughly half a millimetre apart, interrupted by pores that deliver sweat. Fingerprints are the skin's pattern of those ridges, and they are older than any use we have found for them: they were fixed in the womb, they survive burns and abrasion by regrowing from below, and they leave a faint film of oil and sweat on nearly everything you touch.

The ridges take shape between roughly the tenth and twenty-fourth weeks of pregnancy. The ridge pattern follows the basal layer of the skin, where the fingertip is buckling as it grows, and it is shaped by details such as the swelling of the fetal "volar pads" and the flow of amniotic fluid. Because tiny differences in conditions inside each womb tip the outcome, even [[Monozygotic twins|identical twins]], who share one [[DNA]] sequence, end up with prints that resemble each other but never match. Genes set the broad family of patterns, yet not the fine detail, which is why your fingerprints are partly inherited and partly accident.

^ Some people are born without any. [[Adermatoglyphia]], caused by mutations in the SMARCAD1 gene, leaves fingertips smooth and has been called "immigration delay disease" because of the border trouble it causes.

## What the ridges are for

The textbook answer is grip, and it is at best half right. Experiments on friction show that ridges actually reduce contact area on smooth surfaces, which can lower grip rather than raise it. A better case is that ridges are sensory amplifiers. Under each ridge sit [[Dermal papillae|dermal papillae]] and receptors such as [[Meissner's corpuscle|Meissner's corpuscles]], and when a fingertip slides across a texture the ridges vibrate, concentrating fine mechanical signals onto the nerve endings. A 2009 study led by Jean-Louis Thonnard's group at the Université catholique de Louvain found that ridges let touch pick up detail finer than the ridge spacing itself. Ridges also wick moisture, which may help keep the surface from slipping when wet.

Primates are not the only animals with them. The [[Koala]] has prints so human-like that they have confused forensic analysts, even though koalas descend from a lineage separated from ours by well over 100 million years. This is convergent evolution, and it suggests the pattern solves a real problem in handling branches and bark.

## From curiosity to evidence

People pressed fingertips into clay long before anyone thought of identification; ancient Babylonian and Chinese records show fingerprints as marks of a signature. The scientific idea arrived in the 1870s. In Japan the Scottish physician [[Henry Faulds]] published a note in Nature in 1880 proposing prints for identifying criminals, after noticing prints on ancient pottery and helping solve a case of a sooty handprint. The same year, [[William Herschel (administrator)|William Herschel]], a British official in Bengal, claimed he had been using handprints on contracts since 1858 to deter fraud. [[Francis Galton]] then gave the field its statistics in his 1892 book, estimating the chance of two prints matching at about one in 64 billion.

Classification followed. Edward Henry, with Indian police officers Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, devised the [[Henry classification system]], adopted by Scotland Yard in 1901. In the United States, the 1903 [[Will West case]] at Leavenworth, in which two unrelated inmates of near-identical appearance were distinguished only by their prints, helped to end reliance on body measurements.

::: timeline
1880 :: Faulds proposes fingerprints for identifying criminals in Nature
1892 :: Galton publishes Finger Prints, with a classification scheme
1901 :: Scotland Yard adopts the Henry system
1903 :: Will West case helps retire body-measurement identification
1999 :: FBI's computerised IAFIS goes live
:::

Today a trace left at a crime scene is often a [[Latent print development|latent print]], invisible until dusted, fumed with cyanoacrylate or chemically treated. Computers now search millions of records through an [[Automated fingerprint identification system]]. Yet the method is not infallible: in 2004, the FBI wrongly tied Oregon lawyer Brandon Mayfield to the Madrid train bombings, a failure examined in the [[Mayfield case]], and the lasting lesson is that a smudged partial print is a judgement, not a measurement.
