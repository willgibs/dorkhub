When a large whale dies at sea, its body often sinks, and a carcass of 30 to 40 tonnes can fall through thousands of metres of cold, dark water until it lands on the seafloor. For most of the deep ocean, food is a thin drizzle of dead plankton and fecal specks, so a whale arriving all at once is like a truck of groceries dropped on a desert. A single gray whale carcass delivers roughly the amount of organic carbon that normally rains onto a patch of seabed the size of a tennis court over about 2,000 years. Life converges on it from kilometres around, and the feast can last longer than a human lifetime.

The phenomenon was first documented by accident. In 1987, the submersible Alvin, piloted during a dive led by Craig Smith, came across a nearly intact gray whale skeleton at about 1,240 metres in the [[Santa Cruz Basin]] off California, crawling with unfamiliar animals. Before that, whale falls were a hypothesis at best; afterwards, researchers began deliberately sinking carcasses to watch the process unfold. Smith and colleagues later tracked experimental falls over years, and found that the sequence unfolds in distinct stages.

^ Whale falls are spaced so thinly that larvae of some specialist species may need to drift or hop across hundreds of kilometres of barren seabed to find the next one.

## Three courses of a very long meal

First come the mobile scavengers. [[Hagfish]], [[sleeper shark]]s, rattail fish and crabs strip the soft tissue, and the flesh of a large whale can be reduced to bone in months to a couple of years. The stripped skeleton is then colonised by a second wave: dense mats of bacteria and crustaceans, polychaete worms and snails feeding on the lipid-rich sediment and bone surfaces, an "enrichment-opportunist" stage lasting years.

The long final stage is the one that makes whale falls strange. Whale bones are up to 60 percent lipid by weight in some species, and as the oil seeps out, [[Sulfate-reducing bacteria]] break it down without oxygen, producing hydrogen sulfide. That sulfide supports [[Chemosynthesis]]: bacteria that harvest energy from chemicals rather than sunlight, and in turn feed mussels, clams and tubeworms that host them. This "sulphophilic" stage can persist for decades, and models for the largest carcasses suggest up to half a century or more.

::: steps
Descent :: Carcass sinks, often arriving intact on the seabed.
Scavenging :: Sharks, hagfish and crabs strip flesh in months to two years.
Enrichment :: Worms, snails and crustaceans exploit bone and fatty sediment for years.
Sulphophilic :: Sulfide-eating bacteria support mussels and clams for decades.
Reef :: Bare mineral remains offer a hard surface for later colonists.
:::

## The zombie worms

In 2002, Robert Vrijenhoek and Greg Rouse at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute described [[Osedax]], from the Latin for "bone-devourer," found on a whale skeleton about 2,890 metres down in Monterey Canyon. The females are small worms whose bodies sprout feathery red plumes into the water, while root-like tissue burrows into the bone. They have no mouth or gut; symbiotic bacteria in the roots digest the lipids and collagen inside the bone for them. The males are microscopic and live inside the females' tubes, sometimes dozens to a single female.

Osedax is not alone in drawing on the fall. Researchers have catalogued well over 100 species at whale falls, including some that appear to be specialists and others shared with [[Hydrothermal vent]] and cold-seep communities. This overlap has led to the "stepping stone" hypothesis, that whale falls helped chemosynthetic animals disperse between vents across the ocean's barren stretches, though the idea remains debated. Genetic dating hinted that Osedax diverged tens of millions of years ago, and fossil traces of their borings have been found in plesiosaur and sea turtle bones, suggesting bone-eating worms predate whales themselves.

^ Some Osedax borings have been reported in Cretaceous marine reptile bones, long before the first whales evolved.

## Why they matter now

Industrial whaling killed millions of large whales in the twentieth century, and many scientists argue that this dramatically reduced the number of falls reaching the deep. Fewer falls could have meant local extinctions or isolation for specialist species, though the effect is hard to measure. Whale falls also store carbon: a large carcass buries tonnes of it on the seafloor, so recovering whale populations may slowly help rebuild both the oases and a small carbon sink.
