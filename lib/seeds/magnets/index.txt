A magnet is any object that produces a magnetic field, an invisible influence that tugs on iron, nickel, cobalt and a few other materials. Scatter [[iron filings]] on paper over a bar magnet and the field draws itself: the grains line up in arcs that loop from the north pole round to the south, packed tightly near the poles and thinning in between. That picture is the best portrait of a force that has no color, no smell and no contact. Magnets are older than writing about them: pieces of naturally magnetized rock called [[Lodestone|lodestones]] were described by Greek thinkers around 600 BC, and the word likely comes from Magnesia, a region of Greece. Today magnets spin in every electric motor, store data in hard drives, steer particles in accelerators and hold notes to refrigerators.

^ The sharpest lodestone magnets are made when lightning strikes iron ore, which is the leading explanation for how natural magnets form at all.

## Where the pull comes from

Magnetism begins with electrons. Each electron behaves like a tiny spinning magnet, and in most materials these cancel in pairs. In [[Ferromagnetism|ferromagnetic]] metals such as iron, an effect of quantum mechanics called exchange interaction makes neighboring atoms prefer to align their spins, so they add up instead of cancelling. The alignment is not uniform across a whole lump of iron: it breaks into patches called [[Magnetic domain|magnetic domains]], each typically microns to millimeters wide, each pointing its own way. An unmagnetized nail is a patchwork whose domains cancel out; magnetizing it means persuading the domains to line up, either by stroking it with a magnet or by placing it inside a coil of current-carrying wire.

::: steps
Atoms :: Unpaired electron spins make each iron atom a tiny magnet.
Alignment :: Exchange interaction locks neighboring spins in the same direction.
Domains :: Aligned regions form patches that point in random directions.
Magnetizing :: An outside field makes domains aligned with it grow and others shrink.
Result :: Domains line up; the whole object now has a north and a south pole.
:::

Heat undoes all this. Above the [[Curie temperature]], 770 degrees Celsius for iron, thermal jostling overwhelms the alignment and the metal stops being a magnet. Strong blows can also scramble domains, which is why dropping a magnet repeatedly weakens it.

## Strength, and the stranger magnetisms

For centuries the best magnets were hardened steel. The modern leap came in 1983 with the [[Neodymium magnet]], an alloy of neodymium, iron and boron developed independently at Sumitomo in Japan and General Motors in the United States. A fingernail-sized one can lift several kilograms, and swallowed pairs can pinch through intestinal walls, a real medical hazard. Next to them, an [[Electromagnet]] has the advantage of being switchable: the research magnets of the Nijmegen high-field lab reach well over 30 tesla, about 600,000 times Earth's field.

Everything is magnetic to some degree, not just iron. Water, wood and living tissue show [[Diamagnetism]], a feeble repulsion from magnetic fields. In 1997 [[Andre Geim]] and Michael Berry floated a live frog in a 16-tesla field at Nijmegen in the Netherlands: the field is so strong that the repulsion in the frog's own water balanced gravity, and the frog was reportedly unharmed. Geim shared a Nobel Prize in 2010, for graphene rather than frogs, but the levitation earned him an Ig Nobel in 2000.

^ Even a Venus flytrap's electrical activity produces a magnetic field, detected in 2021 with sensitive atomic magnetometers, though it is about a billionth of Earth's.

## The magnet underfoot

The biggest magnet most people meet is the planet. [[Earth's magnetic field]] is generated by swirling liquid iron in the outer core, a self-sustaining process known as the [[Geodynamo]]. It is no static bar: the poles wander and, at irregular intervals averaging a few hundred thousand years, flip completely in a [[Magnetic reversal]], most recently about 780,000 years ago. The field also funnels charged particles from the solar wind toward the poles, where they excite the air into the [[Northern lights]]. The [[Compass]], first documented in China as a lodestone spoon in the Han period and used for navigation by the 11th century, simply reads that field.

Every magnet ever found has two poles; cut one in half and each piece grows a new pair. A [[Magnetic monopole]], a lone north or south, has been predicted by several theories but never observed, and its absence remains one of physics' loose threads.
