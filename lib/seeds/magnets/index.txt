A magnet is any object that creates a magnetic field and pulls on, or pushes against, certain other materials without touching them. The oldest magnets were found, not made: chunks of the black iron ore [[Magnetite]] called [[Lodestone]], which Greek writers around 600 BCE said could make iron move, and which Chinese texts later described as stones that "love" iron. Today magnets sit in hard drives, headphones, MRI scanners, wind turbines and the door of nearly every refrigerator. Yet the basic behaviour is the one the ancients saw: two ends, one that seeks north, one that seeks south, and an invisible reach between them.

## Two poles, never one

Every magnet has a north-seeking pole and a south-seeking pole, and unlike poles attract while like poles repel. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not isolate the poles; each piece grows a fresh north and south, and cutting again repeats the trick, down to the scale of single atoms. Physicists have searched for a lone pole, a [[Magnetic monopole]], since Paul Dirac showed in 1931 that quantum mechanics permits one. Cosmic-ray detectors and particle colliders have found none, and a Stanford detector run by Blas Cabrera recorded one suggestive signal on 14 February 1982 that was never repeated.

^ Pierre Curie's 1894 comment that monopoles seemed possible in theory is why the search began; Dirac's later argument explained why electric charge comes in fixed units.

The pattern of the push and pull is mapped by the [[Magnetic field]]. Sprinkle [[Iron filings]] on paper over a bar magnet and each grain becomes a tiny magnet itself, lining up along the field: loops sweep from the north pole round to the south, and the filings crowd thickest at the poles where the field is strongest. Michael Faraday treated such patterns as real physical lines in space, an idea that grew into modern field theory.

## Why iron, and why only some things

The magnetism of a magnet comes from electrons, which behave like minuscule compass needles because of a quantum property called spin. In most materials the needles cancel. In iron, nickel and cobalt, neighbouring atoms lock their spins parallel through a quantum exchange effect, producing [[Ferromagnetism]]. Even then a lump of iron is usually not a magnet, because it splits into [[Magnetic domain]]s, patches microscopic to visible in size, each fully magnetized but pointing in different directions. Stroking the iron with a magnet, or placing it in a strong field, swings domains into alignment and the metal becomes magnetic.

Heat undoes the order. Above the [[Curie temperature]], named for Pierre Curie, who studied it in the 1890s, thermal jostling overwhelms the alignment and the metal turns only weakly magnetic. Hammering or dropping a magnet also scrambles domains, which is why old magnets weaken. Modern [[Neodymium magnet]]s, invented in 1982 at General Motors and Sumitomo, pack a neodymium-iron-boron crystal so resistant to demagnetizing that a coin-sized piece can lift many kilograms.

## From compass needle to planet

Magnetized needles floating in water or hung on threads became the [[Compass]], used for navigation in China and Europe by about the 12th century. In 1600 [[William Gilbert]], physician to Elizabeth I, floated a lodestone sphere model, a "terrella", and showed that a small needle dipped and pointed across it just as compasses behave on Earth. His conclusion was that the planet itself is a giant magnet. We now know it is not a permanent one: [[Earth's magnetic field]] is produced by swirling molten iron in the outer core, and it has flipped polarity many times, most recently about 780,000 years ago.

The field shields us by deflecting charged particles from the Sun; those caught and funnelled toward the poles collide with the upper atmosphere and glow as the [[Northern lights]]. In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted noticed a current deflecting a compass needle, revealing that electricity and magnetism are linked. That discovery gave us the [[Electromagnet]], a coil whose field switches on and off, and with it motors, generators and transformers.

^ Some migrating animals, including birds and sea turtles, are thought to sense Earth's field, though the receptor is still debated.
