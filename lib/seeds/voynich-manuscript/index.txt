The Voynich manuscript is a small, thick book of calfskin vellum, filled with elegant, flowing script that no one can read. Its pages are crowded with illustrations just as baffling: plants that match no known species, naked women bathing in green liquid and connected by tubes, circular diagrams of stars, and rosettes of nested circles. The writing uses roughly twenty to thirty distinct glyphs, with no corrections and a smooth, practiced flow, as if the scribe knew exactly what to write. Since the Polish-born dealer [[Wilfrid Voynich]] bought it in 1912 in Italy, it has become the most studied unreadable book in the world.

## A trail of owners

The documented history begins in Prague. A letter of 1665 sent by [[Georg Baresch]], an alchemist, to the Jesuit polymath [[Athanasius Kircher]] in Rome describes an unreadable book he had owned for years and begged Kircher to decipher. A later letter, dated 1666 and attached to the manuscript, records it being given to Kircher by Johannes Marcus Marci, who stated that [[Rudolf II]], Holy Roman Emperor, had once paid 600 ducats for it, believing it to be the work of [[Roger Bacon]]. That attribution is now considered unsupported, since Bacon died in the 13th century. Marci's account is secondhand and cannot be verified.

After Kircher, the book vanished for over two centuries, probably in the library of the Jesuit college at Frascati, until Voynich acquired it. He spent years promoting it and sending photostats to cryptographers. After his death his widow sold it on, and in 1969 it was donated to Yale, where it rests in the [[Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library]] as MS 408.

## What the text does, and doesn't do

The oddity is that the text looks like language. [[Zipf's law]], the pattern in which a few words are very common and most are rare, holds in the Voynich text, which is hard to fake by random typing. Yet the words behave strangely: they often repeat two or three times in a row, rarely exceed ten letters, and show far less variation than natural-language words. Some glyphs appear only at the starts of lines or the ends of words. This statistical strangeness is why it resists both easy verdicts: it is too structured to be nonsense, yet too odd to match any known language.

The most celebrated codebreakers failed. [[William Friedman]], who broke Japan's PURPLE machine cipher, led a group in the 1940s and concluded it might be an artificial language rather than a [[Cipher]]. The sections have names modern scholars gave them: a botanical or [[Herbal]] section, an astronomical one, a [[Balneology]] section of bathing women, a section of apparent recipes, and pages of fold-out charts.

## Rival explanations

::: theories
Enciphered natural language :: A real language, scrambled by a cipher system :: disputed
Constructed language :: An invented language, perhaps with its own grammar :: disputed
Hoax :: Meaningless text produced to deceive a buyer :: disputed
Glossolalia or outsider art :: Automatic writing, like [[Glossolalia]] on paper :: disputed
:::

The hoax theory has real support. In 2004 Gordon Rugg showed that a card grille method could produce text with similar statistics, and some argue the sheer difficulty of decoding implies there is nothing to decode. Opponents note that the book's hundreds of pages and consistent internal structure would have been a huge effort for a hoaxer in the 15th century. Many claimed solutions have appeared, from Hebrew to Old Turkic to Proto-Romance, none accepted by scholars. A decipherment would need to yield readable text, explain the statistics, and be reproducible by others. Until one does, the book remains a work like the modern [[Codex Seraphinianus]] in spirit, but with a medieval pedigree: either a message nobody can read, or a performance with no message at all.
