For most of human history the giant squid was a rumor with tentacles. Sailors told of sea monsters, and the [[Kraken]] of Scandinavian lore may owe something to it, but bodies were scarce: a carcass on a beach, a tangle of arms in a trawl net, pieces vomited by whales. The animal itself, [[Architeuthis]] to biologists, is a [[Cephalopod]] with eight arms, two much longer feeding tentacles, and a body plan that looks improbable until you see it scaled up. Large specimens are measured at around 12 metres including the tentacles, though most of that is arm and tentacle rather than bulk. The mantle, the muscular torso, is usually only about two metres.

Its eye is the headline. In specimens, the eyeball reaches about 27 centimetres across, roughly the size of a dinner plate and about eleven times the width of a human eye, which measures around 2.4 centimetres. No other animal known has an eye bigger. Beneath that dark gaze, the skin is flecked with [[Chromatophore]] cells, tiny sacs of pigment that expand and contract to change its colour.

^ A giant squid's eye is so large that it could not fit on a postcard drawn at human scale; the lens alone can be the size of an orange.

## Why such big eyes?

The deep ocean is nearly lightless, and the question is what a squid needs to see there. A 2012 study by Dan-Eric Nilsson and colleagues, published in Current Biology, offered an answer. Computer models suggested that the giant eye is not for seeing prey in the dark at all. It is tuned to detect one thing: the faint glow of [[Bioluminescence]] stirred up by a [[Sperm whale]] swimming through the water. A bigger eye lets the squid notice a whale's approach from far more than a hundred metres away, long enough to flee. Prey, by contrast, is close enough to be seen with a much smaller eye, so the extra size would be wasted on hunting.

This makes the squid and its main predator a matched pair. Sperm whales dive for squid, and their stomachs have supplied most specimens that science has ever handled. Circular scars from squid suckers, studded with tiny toothed rings, scar the skin of sperm whales, evidence of fights that no human has watched.

## Seeing one alive

Stranded animals had been studied since the nineteenth century. Newfoundland fishermen in the 1870s supplied several, and the naturalist Moses Harvey helped bring them to wide attention. Yet a living animal in its own habitat resisted every attempt. The breakthrough came on 30 September 2004, when a team led by [[Tsunemi Kubodera]] of Japan's National Science Museum, near the [[Ogasawara Islands]], photographed a giant squid attacking baited tackle at about 900 metres. Over the day the camera took more than 500 images. In 2012, Kubodera's team, working with Discovery Channel and Japan's broadcaster NHK, filmed one in its natural habitat at around 630 metres from a submersible.

Inside, the animal is odd too. Its flesh is held buoyant by ammonium chloride, a body fluid that is lighter than seawater and tastes of the chemical; this [[Ammonia buoyancy]] trick lets it hang in the water without swimming, and it is also why the meat is unpalatable. A parrot-like beak and a ribbon of teeth called the [[Radula]] shred prey, and the balance organs known as [[Statocyst]]s tell the squid which way is up, and are read by scientists to estimate age. Counting rings in them suggests lifespans of only about five years, short for so large an animal.

Genetic studies have found that giant squid across the globe are remarkably alike, possibly one species in all oceans. A related but distinct giant, the [[Colossal squid]], is heavier but belongs to another family. Cousins such as [[Octopuses]] share the same basic cephalopod design of a brain wrapped around the throat, three hearts and blue blood.
