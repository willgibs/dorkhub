Time travel is the idea of moving through time at a rate other than the ordinary one, or in a direction other than forward. Stated that way it splits into two very different questions. Travel to the future is not hypothetical: it happens, measurably, every day, in clocks on aircraft, on the International Space Station and in orbit over your head. Travel to the past is a different matter, a claim that sits at the edge of what the equations of physics permit, and nobody has come close to demonstrating it. The word "time machine" entered literature with H. G. Wells's novella [[The Time Machine|The Time Machine]] in 1895, but the physics that makes the forward half respectable arrived a decade later.

## The forward road is already open

In 1905 Einstein's [[Special relativity|special relativity]] showed that a moving clock runs slow relative to a stationary one, an effect called [[Time dilation|time dilation]]. At everyday speeds it is absurdly small, but it has been measured directly. In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew atomic clocks around the world on commercial airliners, and the clocks came back out of step with those left at the US Naval Observatory by a few hundred nanoseconds, matching prediction in the [[Hafele–Keating experiment|Hafele–Keating experiment]]. Cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev, after 803 days in orbit across several missions, returned about 0.02 seconds into the future of everyone he left behind.

^ Krikalev launched as a citizen of the Soviet Union and landed in a different country: his 1991 mission outlasted the USSR itself.

Gravity bends time too. [[General relativity|General relativity]] predicts that clocks deeper in a gravitational field tick more slowly, so a clock on a mountain ages faster than one at sea level. The [[Global Positioning System|GPS]] constellation depends on both effects at once: satellite clocks run fast by about 45 microseconds a day because they sit higher in Earth's gravity, and slow by about 7 because of their orbital speed, a net gain of 38 microseconds per day. Left uncorrected, position errors would grow by roughly 10 kilometres daily, so engineers build the correction into the satellites' clock rates before launch.

::: steps
Satellite height :: Orbit at 20,200 km means weaker gravity, so clocks run faster
Satellite speed :: About 3.9 km/s orbital motion slows clocks slightly
Net result :: Clocks gain roughly 38 microseconds per day
Correction :: Clock frequency is offset before launch to cancel the drift
:::

## Going further than a few seconds

The extreme version of gravitational time dilation lives near [[Black holes|black holes]]. Close to the event horizon, an outside observer sees clocks slow toward a standstill, so a traveller who hovered there and returned could leave centuries behind with little aging. The classic version is the [[Twin paradox|twin paradox]]: one twin cruises away at near light speed and comes home younger than the sibling who stayed. It looks like a contradiction only until you notice the travelling twin must turn around, breaking the symmetry between them. The cost is energy: reaching one year of future per day of travel needs speeds past 99.99 percent of light speed.

## The backward road is a bet

General relativity does not forbid travel to the past outright. Its equations allow [[Closed timelike curve|closed timelike curves]], paths through spacetime that loop back to their start. In 1949 [[Kurt Gödel|Kurt Gödel]], Einstein's neighbour at Princeton, found a rotating-universe solution, the [[Gödel metric|Gödel metric]], in which such loops exist everywhere. Our universe does not appear to rotate, and other proposals, such as traversable [[Wormhole|wormholes]] held open by exotic matter with negative energy density, require substances no one has found. Stephen Hawking's chronology protection conjecture suggests that quantum effects would destroy any time machine just as it was about to switch on, though it remains unproven.

Even if the loops exist, logic imposes rules. The [[Grandfather paradox|grandfather paradox]], in which a traveller prevents their own birth, is the best-known problem. One proposed escape is the [[Novikov self-consistency principle|Novikov self-consistency principle]], formulated by Igor Novikov in the 1980s, under which only histories without contradiction can occur, so the traveller's gun jams or their aim fails. Another is branching timelines, in which the past you change belongs to a different universe than the one you left. Which, if either, is right, nobody can say, because no one has a time machine to test it.
