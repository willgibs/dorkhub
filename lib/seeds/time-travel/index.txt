Time travel is the idea of moving between points in time other than at the usual one-second-per-second pace, and it comes in two very different flavors. One is entirely real: your clock can run slow relative to someone else's, so that you arrive in their future having aged less. The other, travel into the past, is a staple of fiction that turns up as a strange but tolerated possibility in the equations of Einstein, and no experiment has ever come close to it. Between those two lies one of the most fruitful borderlands in physics, where the nature of cause and effect itself is put on trial.

The literary story starts late. H. G. Wells's [[The Time Machine]] (1895) gave readers a vehicle you could steer through time, and nearly every later tale borrows its furniture. Yet the physics came first in a quiet way: in 1905 Einstein's [[Special relativity]] showed that time is not a universal metronome, and the vehicle turned out to be speed.

## Travel to the future is real

[[Time dilation]] comes in two forms. Under special relativity, a moving clock ticks slower than a stationary one; under [[General relativity]], a clock deeper in a gravitational well ticks slower than one higher up. In October 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew cesium atomic clocks around the world on commercial airliners and found them out of step with the reference clocks at the U.S. Naval Observatory by tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, matching prediction (the [[Hafele–Keating experiment]]).

^ Satellites in the [[Global Positioning System]] run about 38 microseconds per day fast after both effects are combined. Without correction, position errors would grow by roughly 10 kilometers daily.

The effects are tiny at ordinary speeds but real. The cosmonaut [[Sergei Krikalev]] spent about 803 days in orbit across his career, mostly aboard Mir, and came home roughly 0.02 seconds younger than if he had stayed on the ground, the largest accumulated time shift of any human. A year on the International Space Station shaves off a few milliseconds. To do better you need either near-light speeds or an enormous gravitational pull, and near a [[Black holes|black hole]] the slowdown becomes extreme: at the horizon, as seen from far away, a clock appears to stop altogether.

::: figures
38 μs :: daily net clock gain of GPS satellites
0.02 s :: Krikalev's approximate time shift after about 803 days in orbit
~10 km :: daily GPS error if relativity were ignored
:::

## Travel to the past: the equations allow some, and nobody has used one

General relativity describes spacetime that can bend so far that a path loops back to its own starting point: a [[Closed timelike curve]], a worldline that returns to the same event. In 1949 Kurt Gödel, then a colleague of Einstein at Princeton, found an exact solution, the [[Gödel metric]], describing a rotating universe in which such loops exist. In 1974 Frank Tipler showed that an infinitely long, rapidly rotating cylinder would do it too. Kip Thorne and colleagues proposed in 1988 that a traversable [[Wormhole]] with one mouth sped up and parked could be a time machine, though it would require exotic matter with negative energy density.

Reasons for doubt are weighty. The known solutions rely on infinite lengths, wrongly shaped universes, or matter nobody has seen. Stephen Hawking proposed the [[Chronology protection conjecture]] in 1992, suggesting that quantum effects, such as a runaway buildup of vacuum energy, would destroy any time machine just as it forms; it remains a conjecture, unproven. Even if closed loops exist, one rule seems to hold: you could never visit a time before the machine was built.

## Paradoxes and what they teach

The famous trouble is the [[Grandfather paradox]]: go back and prevent your own grandparents from meeting, and you could never have existed to go back. Physicists have two main answers. The [[Novikov self-consistency principle]], developed by Igor Novikov in the 1980s, holds that only consistent histories happen, so the gun jams or you miss, and the past was always the one you visited. The alternative is that your trip lands you in a branching universe, as in the many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics, where changing the past just creates a different timeline. Work by David Deutsch (1991) models the second option mathematically.

::: theories
Self-consistency :: Only histories without contradiction occur; the past you visit was always yours :: partly
Branching timelines :: Visiting the past spawns a new history, leaving yours untouched :: partly
Chronology protection :: Physics always prevents time machines from forming :: disputed
:::
