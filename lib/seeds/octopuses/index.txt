An octopus is a mollusc that gave up its shell, and almost everything remarkable about it follows from that bargain. Its relatives, the clams and snails, are armoured and slow; the octopus is a soft, boneless body of about 500 million neurons that can pour itself through any gap larger than its beak, the only hard part it has. There are around 300 known species, from the thumb-sized Californian pygmy octopus to the giant Pacific octopus, which can span several metres and weigh tens of kilograms. They live in every ocean, from tide pools to the deep sea, and almost all live alone and die young: most species survive only one to five years.

An octopus belongs to the [[cephalopod|Cephalopod]] class, the same group as cuttlefish, nautiluses and the [[giant squid|Giant squid]], but it is the group's great improviser. Its blood is blue, carrying oxygen on copper-based [[hemocyanin|Hemocyanin]] instead of iron-based haemoglobin, an arrangement that works well in cold, low-oxygen water. Three hearts drive it: two push blood through the gills and one serves the rest of the body, and that systemic heart actually stops beating while the animal swims, which is one reason octopuses prefer crawling to jetting.

## A body that thinks in pieces

The front-page claim holds up: about two-thirds of an octopus's neurons sit in its arms, not its central brain. Each arm carries a chain of ganglia that can taste, touch and decide, and experiments on severed arms show they keep reaching and grasping reflexively for a while. Work on [[arm autonomy|Octopus arm autonomy]] suggests the brain issues broad intentions ("go there", "grab that") while the arm works out the details, which makes sense for a body with no skeleton and effectively infinite degrees of freedom. The suckers themselves are covered in chemoreceptors, so an octopus tastes what it touches.

^ Octopus arms can regrow after loss, and the brain does not map each arm's position the way a human brain maps a hand.

Skin is the other great organ. Thousands of [[chromatophores|Chromatophore]], sacs of pigment each stretched open by tiny muscles, let an octopus change colour and texture in a fraction of a second. Yet octopuses are colour-blind by standard tests, so how they match backgrounds so exactly remains a live puzzle; one hypothesis is that light-sensitive proteins in the skin itself help. The [[mimic octopus|Mimic octopus]] of Indonesian waters goes further, apparently imitating sea snakes and lionfish, a famous case of [[mimicry|Mimicry]], though how much of it is deliberate imitation is debated.

## Rewriting their own recipe

Most animals copy their genetic instructions faithfully into RNA. Coleoid cephalopods, which include octopuses, squid and cuttlefish, edit their RNA extensively, altering the proteins their cells build without changing the DNA. A 2017 study led by Joshua Rosenthal and Eli Eisenberg found this [[RNA editing|RNA editing]] hits neural genes at unusually high rates, and the animals seem to trade slower genome evolution for flexibility. In the cold-water Antarctic octopus, editing appears to help nerve proteins function at low temperatures.

## Hunters, sleepers and dreamers

Octopuses are predators of crabs, clams and fish, and they drill shells, jet from ambush and sometimes carry coconut shell halves as portable shelters. On [[coral reefs|Coral reefs]] of the Red Sea, researchers led by Eduardo Sampaio reported in 2024 that some octopuses cooperate with reef fish, with fish groupers and goatfish hunting alongside them, and the octopus sometimes punches fish that slack off. Defence comes from ink, camouflage and, in the [[blue-ringed octopus|Blue-ringed octopus]], [[tetrodotoxin|Tetrodotoxin]], a venom that can kill a person.

They also sleep in two states. A quiet phase has pale skin and pinprick pupils; an active phase, described in a 2023 study from the Okinawa Institute led by Sam Reiter, features rapid colour flicker across the skin. Researchers wonder whether this resembles [[REM sleep and dreams|Dreams]], and [[octopus sleep|Octopus sleep]] studies hint at a convergence with vertebrates, though what the animal experiences is unknown.
