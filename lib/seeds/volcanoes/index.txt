On the morning of 27 August 1883, the island of [[1883 eruption of Krakatoa|Krakatoa]] in the Sunda Strait, between Java and Sumatra, exploded with a sound that was heard 4,800 kilometres away. The blast threw ash and rock high into the sky, and much of the island then collapsed into the sea. It was one of the loudest sounds in recorded history, and its ash turned sunsets red around the world for months.

## Sound, sea and a flooded crater

Barometers at observatories around the world recorded the pressure wave seven times as it circled the globe. Most of the roughly 36,000 deaths came from the tsunamis that the explosions and collapse sent into coastal towns on Java and Sumatra. Afterwards the sea flooded the hollow left by the collapse, forming a [[Caldera]] that remains. A new cone, Anak Krakatau, rose from the same waters in 1927, and it has erupted repeatedly since.
^ Floating [[Pumice|pumice]] from the blast crossed the Indian Ocean and washed up on the African coast.

## What drives the blast

[[Magma]] is rock that has melted deep underground, and it rises because it is less dense than the rock around it. Most magma, though, rises from shallow chambers, and it is the gas in those chambers that makes an eruption violent. Some kimberlite eruptions carry [[diamonds|Diamonds]] up from more than 150 kilometres down, and their pipes are where many diamond mines are dug.
::: steps
Rise :: Buoyant magma climbs through cracks toward shallower depths.
Bubbles :: Dissolved gas leaves the melt as pressure falls, and each bubble swells.
Jam :: Thick magma traps the gas, so pressure builds beneath a plug.
Blast :: The plug gives way, and the mixture bursts upward as ash and gas.
:::

How violent an eruption becomes depends on how easily its gas escapes. Runny basaltic magma lets gas out quietly and builds fountains and lava flows, while sticky, silica-rich magma can sustain a towering column that spreads ash for kilometres, a style called a [[Plinian eruption]], named after the Roman writer Pliny the Younger. Volcanologists often judge a volcano's danger by which of these styles it is most likely to produce.

## Buried in AD 79

In AD 79 [[Mount Vesuvius]] erupted above the Bay of Naples and buried [[Pompeii]] under a thick layer of ash. Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and later wrote about the eruption in letters to the historian Tacitus. The ash fell for hours before the worst arrived: [[pyroclastic flows|Pyroclastic flow]], dense clouds of hot gas and rock, raced down the slopes and reached the towns on the coast, killing many who had stayed behind.
^ Pliny the Younger was about seventeen, and his letters remain the main eyewitness account of the eruption.

## Lightning and mudflows

Ash plumes make their own [[Lightning]]. Particles of ash colliding and rubbing inside the rising column build up electric charge, and the cloud can flash like a thunderstorm. Scientists call this volcanic lightning, and it is most common in the ash clouds of explosive eruptions rather than in lava flows.

The threat that outlasts the eruption is the [[Lahar|lahar]], a flood of volcanic mud and water that can run down river valleys long after the ash has stopped falling. Lahars have killed many people far from the summit, including in the 1985 eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia, where the mudflows destroyed the town of Armero. The town sat many kilometres downstream, in the valley the mudflows followed.
