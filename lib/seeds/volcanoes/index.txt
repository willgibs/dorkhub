A volcano is a place where molten rock, gas and ash escape from inside a planet. On Earth the picture most people carry, a cone with a plume over it, is only the visible tenth of the story. Volcanoes build islands, bury cities, cool the climate for years and, over billions of years, produced the air and oceans. They also range in manner from the gentle lava lakes of Kilauea to explosions that can be heard thousands of kilometres away.

## Where the magma really goes

Most of Earth's volcanism is invisible. Roughly three-quarters of all magma erupted each year comes out along the [[Mid-ocean ridge|mid-ocean ridges]], a seam some 65,000 kilometres long that winds through every ocean. There, plates pull apart and the mantle beneath rises and partly melts, filling the gap with fresh basalt. The seafloor is made this way, continuously, and no one sees it happen. Cold seawater circulating through the new rock re-emerges as 350-degree fluid at [[Black smoker|black smokers]], whose chimneys were first photographed in 1977 near the Galapagos.

The rest is mostly the famous kind. Where one plate dives beneath another, water carried down lowers the melting point of the mantle above, and the resulting magma feeds the volcanic arcs of the [[Pacific Ring of Fire]], which holds about 450 volcanoes. This is [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]] at work, and it explains why Japan, the Andes and the Aleutians erupt while most continental interiors do not.

::: compare Ridge volcanism | Arc volcanism
Setting :: Plates pulling apart | One plate sinking under another
Magma :: Runny basalt | Thick, gas-rich, often silica-heavy
Style :: Quiet, effusive, underwater | Often explosive
Visibility :: Almost never seen | Dominates the news
:::

^ Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, one of the few places where a ridge rises above sea level and can be walked on.

## Why some erupt gently and others explode

The difference is mostly viscosity and gas. Runny basalt lets bubbles escape, so lava pours out, as at the [[Hawaiian hotspot]], where a plume of hot mantle under the moving Pacific plate has strung out a chain of volcanoes over 70 million years. Sticky, silica-rich magma traps gas under pressure, like a shaken bottle, until it fragments into ash and pumice. The result can be a [[Pyroclastic flow]], a avalanche of hot gas and debris moving at hundreds of kilometres an hour, which is what killed the people of Herculaneum and [[Pompeii]] in AD 79.

Friction between ash particles in the plume generates charge, and [[Volcanic lightning]] flickers inside the column, seen at Eyjafjallajokull in 2010 and Hunga Tonga in 2022.

## Volcanoes and the wider world

Eruptions reach far beyond their slopes. In April 1815 [[Tambora]] in Indonesia produced the largest eruption of recorded history, killing tens of thousands directly, and the sulphate aerosols it lofted cooled the planet enough to cause the [[Year Without a Summer]] in 1816, with crop failures across Europe and North America. The 1883 blast of [[Krakatoa]] was heard some 4,800 kilometres away and generated waves that killed over 30,000 people. Some archaeologists link the [[Minoan eruption]] of Thera, around 1600 BC, to the decline of Bronze Age Crete, though the dating and the link are debated.

Volcanoes also deliver treasure from deep within. [[Kimberlite]] pipes, erupting from over 150 kilometres down, carried diamonds to the surface, and are the source of many of the world's gems. The solar system has its own giants: [[Olympus Mons]] on Mars is about 22 kilometres high, roughly two and a half times Everest's elevation above sea level.
