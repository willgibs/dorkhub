The Carrington Event was the great magnetic storm of early September 1859, one of the most intense ever recorded and still the yardstick for what the Sun could do to a wired world. It began with a flash seen by the English astronomer [[Richard Carrington]], and within a day auroras glowed over the tropics while telegraph lines ran wild.

## A flash over a sunspot

On 1 September, Carrington was sketching sunspots from a projected image of the Sun at his private observatory in Redhill, south of London. At about 11:18 a.m., two patches of brilliant white light burst out inside a large spot group, drifted across it and faded within five minutes. He ran for a witness and returned within a minute to find them dimming. Richard Hodgson saw it independently in Highgate. It was the first solar flare ever recorded, and a rare kind: a [[white-light flare|White-light flare]], bright enough to show against the Sun's own glare.
^ Solar physicists still count the Sun's turns in Carrington rotations, from one that began on 9 November 1853; the count is now past 2,300.

At [[Kew Observatory]] in Richmond, magnets recording the Earth's field twitched at almost the same moment. That jolt, now called a [[magnetic crochet|Magnetic crochet]], comes from a flare's X-rays boosting electric currents in the sunlit upper atmosphere. Carrington noted the coincidence but would not build a theory on one case: one swallow, he wrote, does not make a summer.

## Red skies and live wires

The flash was the visible face of a bigger eruption. The Sun had also flung a vast cloud of magnetized gas toward Earth, an outburst now called a [[coronal mass ejection|Coronal mass ejection]]. It crossed unusually fast, probably because an earlier eruption, which had caused a lesser storm on 28 August, had swept the way clear. Only one crossing has been timed faster since, during the [[August 1972 solar storms]].
::: steps
Flare :: Tangled magnetic fields above the sunspots release energy as light and X-rays
Ejection :: A cloud of magnetized gas crosses the 150 million km to Earth in 17.6 hours
Impact :: If the cloud's field points south, it links with Earth's and pours in energy
Currents :: Electric currents surge through space around Earth and the upper atmosphere, swinging the field below
Induction :: The changing field drives current through the ground and long wires earthed at both ends
:::

Auroras that usually hug the poles were reported as far south as Cuba and Hawaii, glowing as a [[red aurora|Red aurora]] lit by oxygen hundreds of kilometres up. In the Rocky Mountains, gold miners were reportedly woken by the light and started cooking breakfast, thinking it was dawn. In the north-eastern United States, people said they could read a newspaper by it.

The telegraph took the brunt. Across both storms its long wires picked up [[geomagnetically induced currents|Geomagnetically induced current]] that garbled messages, threw sparks and gave operators shocks. On the morning of 2 September, operators in Boston and Portland, Maine, unhooked their batteries and worked for about two hours on the storm's current alone.

## Taking its measure

At Kew and Greenwich, the traces of [[photographic magnetographs|Photographic magnetograph]], drawn by light bounced off a mirror on a hanging magnet, ran off the paper at the storm's height. The best record of the peak came from [[Colaba Observatory]] in Bombay, where observers read their magnets by eye and logged the field's horizontal strength plunging by about 1,600 nanoteslas. Estimates built on that record differ twofold, but even cautious ones rank the storm among the strongest ever measured.
^ A nitrate spike in Greenland ice was once read as a trace of the 1859 storm's particles; later studies argue it was smoke from forest fires.

Tree rings keep a far longer record of the Sun's violence. In 2012 Fusa Miyake's team found a sudden jump in radiocarbon in Japanese cedar rings from 774 to 775 CE. Several such spikes, now called [[Miyake events|Miyake event]], are known, and most researchers blame them on bursts of particles from the Sun, though that is unproven. By one estimate the 774 burst was at least five times stronger than any measured by instruments.

## The next one

Today a storm like 1859's would hit power grids and satellites. A weaker one, the [[March 1989 geomagnetic storm]], collapsed Quebec's grid in under 90 seconds and left six million people without power for about nine hours. The [[solar storm of 2012|Solar storm of 2012]], which some researchers rate as strong as Carrington's, missed Earth only because it erupted about a week too late.
