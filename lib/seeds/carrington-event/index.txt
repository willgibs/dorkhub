On the morning of 1 September 1859, the amateur astronomer [[Richard Carrington|Richard Carrington]] was sketching a huge group of [[Sunspot|sunspots]] from his private observatory at Redhill, south of London, when two patches of intense white light flared up within the group. He watched them brighten for about five minutes before they faded, and he rushed off to find a witness, returning to find the show over. At almost the same time, Richard Hodgson, another English amateur, saw the same flash from his own observatory. It was the first recorded [[Solar flare]], and Carrington suspected at once that it had something to do with what happened next.

What happened next arrived about 17 hours later. Normal transit from Sun to Earth for such an ejection takes three or four days; this one took under a day, a speed that modern researchers attribute to the fact that an earlier eruption had swept the path clear. A [[Coronal mass ejection]], a billion-tonne cloud of magnetized plasma, slammed into Earth's magnetic field and triggered the most intense [[Geomagnetic storm]] on record.

## The night the sky caught fire

The [[Aurora]] that followed was seen from Cuba, Jamaica, Hawaii and Colombia, near the equator. In the Rocky Mountains, gold miners woke and began making breakfast, believing dawn had come, and newspaper accounts claim that people could read print by the light. Auroral glow reflected off the snow. Ships' crews saw red curtains of light spreading across the entire sky, and the colors were described as crimson, green and purple so bright they cast shadows.

^ Ice core nitrate spikes once used as a Carrington marker turned out to be unreliable; scientists later found that nitrate in Greenland ice is a poor storm proxy.

The [[Telegraph]] network, then the most advanced technology on Earth, went haywire. Operators in the United States and Europe reported sparks leaping from equipment, shocks to their hands, and scorched paper. In one famous exchange, two operators, in Boston and Portland, Maine, disconnected their batteries and kept transmitting for about two hours on the current induced by the aurora alone. The mechanism is a [[Geomagnetically induced current]]: a rapidly changing magnetic field drives electric current through long conductors, whether telegraph wires then or power lines now.

1 Sep, ~11:18 :: Carrington and Hodgson independently see a white-light flare
1–2 Sep :: Plasma cloud arrives; telegraphs fail across Europe and North America
2 Sep :: Auroras visible near the tropics; operators work without batteries
:::

## Measuring a storm no one could measure

Magnetometers at [[Kew Observatory]] in London recorded the event, and the magnetic disturbance there swung wildly enough to throw the instrument's trace off its recording paper. From historical records, later studies estimated the storm's strength at Dst of roughly minus 900 nanotesla, though this figure is debated. For comparison, the [[March 1989 geomagnetic storm]] reached about minus 589 and blacked out Quebec's grid in 90 seconds, leaving six million people without power for nine hours.

The question that matters is how often this happens. [[Ice core]] records of nitrate were once thought to carry the signature, a claim later retracted, while radiocarbon spikes tied to a [[Miyake event]] suggest far larger storms in antiquity, around 774 CE, perhaps ten times the energy. Flare activity follows the roughly 11-year [[Solar cycle]], with big storms possible at any phase.

In July 2012, a Carrington-class ejection of similar scale crossed Earth's orbit, missing the planet by about nine days, according to analysis by Daniel Baker and colleagues. Had it hit, insurance estimates by Lloyd's of London have put the potential damage to the US economy alone in the trillions of dollars, a figure that remains an estimate rather than a measurement.

~5 minutes :: duration of the flare Carrington observed
9 days :: how late the July 2012 storm missed Earth
:::
