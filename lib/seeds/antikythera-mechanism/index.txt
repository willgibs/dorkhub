In the spring of 1900, Greek sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the tiny island of Antikythera descended to about 45 metres and found a graveyard of statues: bronze and marble figures, amphorae, glassware, jewellery. Among the salvage hauled up by the [[Antikythera shipwreck|wreck]] expeditions was a shapeless, crusted lump of corroded bronze, easy to overlook beside the sculptures. In May 1902, the archaeologist Valerios Stais noticed that it had split, revealing a gear wheel with triangular teeth. Nothing like it was supposed to exist in the ancient world.

The wreck itself dates to roughly 70–60 BCE, a cargo ship carrying Greek loot, probably bound for Rome. The device inside it was older, and was already an antique when it sank. Its fragments, 82 in all, are now in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.

## What it did

Turn the crank and the machine ran time forward or backward, driving pointers across dials. The front face showed the Sun and Moon against the zodiac and an Egyptian calendar. The Moon pointer's speed varied, as the real Moon's does, through a pin-and-slot arrangement that mimicked its uneven motion. A small ball half painted black showed the lunar phase. On the back, two great spiral dials tracked long-range cycles: the 19-year [[Metonic cycle]], reconciling lunar months with the solar year, and the 223-month [[Saros cycle]] that predicts when [[Lunar eclipse|lunar and solar eclipses]] can recur. Subsidiary dials counted the 76-year [[Callippic cycle]] and a four-year cycle naming the [[Panhellenic Games]], including Olympia and the Naa games at Dodona.

Inscriptions covering the casing, a kind of user's manual in tiny Greek letters, were partly read by [[Computed tomography|X-ray tomography]] in the 2000s, revealing planets named and probably a display of them as well, though the planetary gearing is mostly lost.

## How it was cracked

The physicist and historian [[Derek de Solla Price]] of Yale used gamma radiography in the 1950s–70s and published in 1974 his reconstruction of the gear train, counting teeth on wheels hidden inside the corrosion. He recognised the [[Epicyclic gearing|differential gearing]] used to subtract one rotation from another, something historians had thought was invented only in the sixteenth century. The Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, formed in 2005, brought in Hewlett-Packard's imaging and X-Tek's huge microfocus CT scanner, and the 2006 results in Nature showed the eclipse prediction and the Games dial. In 2021 a UCL team under Tony Freeth proposed a full model of the planetary gearing using concentric tubes.

## Who made it, and why it vanished

No maker is certain. The calendar's month names suggest a Corinthian colony tradition, possibly [[Corinthian colonies|Syracuse]], home of [[Archimedes]], who was said by Cicero to have built a sphere showing the heavens' motions. The eclipse dial uses Babylonian-derived arithmetic that [[Hipparchus]] helped bring into Greek astronomy, though his direct involvement is debated. The lunar model, embedded in the gearing, fits Hipparchus's theory.

The most unsettling fact is the silence afterward. No similar machine survives, and written references to geared astronomical devices are rare: Cicero mentions such spheres, and a [[Parapegma|parapegma]]-like text of star risings is inscribed on the device itself. Gear-driven astronomical clocks reappear in the Islamic world and medieval Europe, only centuries later.
