The Antikythera mechanism is a geared bronze machine from the Greek world, made in the second or early first century BCE, that modelled the sky. Turning a crank drove pointers that tracked the Sun, the Moon and its phases, a calendar of lunar months and coming eclipses. Its corroded remains came from the [[Antikythera wreck]], and it is often called the oldest known analogue computer: no surviving object matches its gearing for over a thousand years.

## Raised by sponge divers

In spring 1900, sponge divers from Symi, waiting out bad weather off Antikythera, found a wreck strewn with bronze and marble statues. They worked it in the canvas suits and copper helmets that [[sponge diving|Sponge diving]] crews had adopted a generation earlier, with Greek navy ships in support. Work stopped in the summer of 1901, after one diver died and two were paralysed by [[decompression sickness|Decompression sickness]]. Among the finds were the bronze [[Antikythera Youth]] and a lump of corroded bronze and wood that went unnoticed until May 1902, when the archaeologist Valerios Stais saw a gearwheel in one of its broken pieces.
^ The first diver down surfaced describing rotting corpses and horses on the seabed; his captain dived himself and came back with a bronze statue's arm.

## A sky on two faces

On the front, pointers swept around a zodiac scale and a ring of Egyptian month names, and a small ball, half pale and half dark, turned to show the Moon's phase. Letters on the zodiac scale were keyed to a [[parapegma|Parapegma]], a star calendar of risings and settings inscribed above and below the dial. Its inscriptions mention the planets too, which it probably showed, though nearly all of that gearing is lost.

The back carried two spiral dials. The upper one counted the 235 months of the [[Metonic cycle]], the 19 years after which the Moon's phases return to the same dates, and a smaller dial beside it tracked the four-year round of the [[Panhellenic Games]], the Olympics among them. The lower spiral held the 223 months of the [[Saros cycle]], after which eclipses repeat, with glyphs marking the months due an eclipse of the Sun or the Moon, and its hour.

## Gears that copy the Moon

The cleverest gearing copies the Moon's uneven pace, faster when it is nearer Earth. Two gears turn on slightly offset axles, and a pin on one rides in a slot on the other, so the Moon pointer speeds up and slows down in turn. The pair rides on a larger turning wheel, an early use of [[epicyclic gearing|Epicyclic gearing]], which swings the whole effect round once every nine years or so, as the Moon's orbit itself slowly turns. The design follows the lunar theory of [[Hipparchus]], who worked on Rhodes, and many of its cycles came from Babylon, where the [[Babylonian astronomical diaries]] logged the night sky for some six centuries.
^ The gear teeth are simple triangles, probably cut by hand, about 1.6 millimetres apart, and not all of them are even.

## Who built it, and who read it

No maker's name survives. Its Corinthian-style month names once pointed scholars to Syracuse, a Corinthian colony and the home of Archimedes; later work favours Epirus in north-western Greece, whose Naa games at Dodona appear on the games dial. Cicero describes [[Archimedes' sphere]], carried off to Rome after Syracuse fell in 212 BCE, and a similar device made by his friend Posidonius, who taught on Rhodes. Neither has been found.

[[Derek de Solla Price]] studied the fragments for two decades, aided by gamma-ray and X-ray images, and called the device a calendar computer. Michael Wright of London's Science Museum later showed Price's gearing to be badly flawed and built working models. In 2005 a team brought an eight-tonne X-ray scanner to Athens, while photographs lit from many directions, a method called [[reflectance transformation imaging|Reflectance transformation imaging]], raised worn letters from the bronze. By 2016 about 3,400 characters had been read, including a guide to the displays.
