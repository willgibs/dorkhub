Fireworks are small chemical machines built to climb, explode and glow on schedule. A typical aerial shell is a paper or plastic sphere packed with dozens to hundreds of pressed pellets called stars, wrapped around a bursting charge, with a time fuse that burns while the shell flies. A lift charge of [[black powder]] at the base throws it from a mortar tube at the speed of a sprinting car; seconds later, hundreds of metres up, the fuse reaches the burst charge and the stars are flung outward in a sphere. Each star carries its own recipe: fuel, oxidiser, binder and a pinch of metal salt that decides its colour.

The thump you hear after the flash is just sound arriving late, since light travels far faster than sound. Watch from a mile away and the delay runs several seconds, which is why a distant display looks like a silent film that is later dubbed.

## Every colour is a metal

Colour in fireworks is atomic bookkeeping. Heat the star and the metal atoms are vaporised and their electrons jump to higher energy levels; when they fall back, each atom gives off light at wavelengths fixed by its element, the same principle as the [[emission spectrum]] of any glowing gas and the [[flame test]] done in school labs. [[Strontium]] salts give red, [[barium]] compounds give green, sodium gives yellow, and copper gives blue. Purple is a cheat: strontium red and copper blue burning together so the eye blends them. White glitter comes from burning aluminium, magnesium or titanium, which glow with broad, hot light rather than a single colour.

^ The same physics paints the [[northern lights]]: oxygen atoms excited by solar particles glow green and red, just as strontium and barium do in a shell.

Those metals were not made on Earth. Barium, strontium, copper and the rest were forged by nuclear fusion in stars and scattered by [[supernovas]], so a green burst is a few hundred million years of stellar history, delivered by the fireworks company.

## Why blue is hard

Blue needs a very narrow window. The best emitter is copper(I) chloride, but it breaks down above roughly 1,200 °C, while a firework star must burn hot enough to stay bright. Too cool and the colour is a dim whisper; too hot and the molecule falls apart and the blue washes out to green or white. Chlorine must also be present, and ordinary oxidisers like potassium nitrate burn too hot to be used freely. Chemists have long worked with compounds such as potassium perchlorate and chlorine donors like PVC, balancing temperature and chlorine supply to keep the copper chloride alive.

::: compare Red | Blue
Emitter :: Strontium chloride | Copper chloride
Heat tolerance :: Forgiving | Falls apart above ~1,200 °C
Brightness :: Strong | Faint
:::

Unlike the cold light of fireflies and deep-sea animals, this is [[bioluminescence]]'s opposite: heat-driven incandescence and atomic emission, glow bought with a furnace rather than an enzyme.

## From Chinese kitchens to modern shows

Gunpowder was discovered in China, probably by alchemists seeking elixirs around the ninth century, and by the Song dynasty it filled bamboo tubes and paper wrappers thrown into festivals. Travellers carried the idea west: accounts credit [[Marco Polo]] among those who described Chinese displays, though the story of him bringing powder home is legend. In Renaissance Italy the art became courtly spectacle, and families such as the [[Ruggieri family]] later took it to Paris. In England, the [[Gunpowder Plot]] of 1605 left a lasting national tradition of bonfires and fireworks every fifth of November.

Colour as we know it arrived in the nineteenth century, when Italian pyrotechnists began adding metal salts, and chlorates made vivid reds and greens possible. Modern shows are fired electronically by computer, with shells timed to music to the millisecond. The classic [[aerial shell]] design has hardly changed, but the choreography has.
