A supernova is the explosion that ends a star's life, a burst so bright that a single dying star can rival the combined light of the hundred billion stars around it. For a few weeks it blazes, then fades over months, leaving behind either a dense stellar corpse or nothing at all but an expanding shell of glowing gas. In the year 1054, Chinese court astronomers recorded a "guest star" in Taurus that was visible in daylight for 23 days and in the night sky for nearly two years. Its wreckage is the [[Crab Nebula|Crab Nebula]], still expanding, still powered by a spinning remnant at its heart. The event is now catalogued as [[SN 1054]].

Astronomers recognize two main routes to catastrophe. In the first, a star more than about eight times the mass of the Sun runs out of fuel; in the second, a small, dead star is pushed past its limit by a companion. Both end the same way: runaway energy that tears the star apart, and the light we see is only a sliver of it.

^ Roughly 99 percent of a core-collapse supernova's energy escapes as [[neutrino|neutrinos]], not light. The visible flash is a rounding error on the real event.

## Collapse of a giant

A massive star lives by fusing lighter elements into heavier ones, stacking them in onion-like layers: hydrogen outside, then helium, carbon, oxygen, silicon, and finally an iron core. Iron is the end of the line, because fusing it consumes energy rather than releasing it. Silicon burning in the final stage of a very massive star lasts only about a day. Once the iron core reaches roughly 1.4 solar masses, electron pressure can no longer hold it up, and it collapses in under a second, shrinking from the size of Earth to a ball some 20 kilometers across.

The core rebounds off its own incompressible density, and the shockwave stalls until a flood of [[neutrino|neutrinos]] revives it. In 1987, detectors in Japan and Ohio caught 24 neutrinos from [[SN 1987A]] in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a few hours before the light arrived, confirming the picture. What remains is a [[neutron star]] if the core is under a few solar masses, or a [[black holes|black hole]] if it is heavier, the collapse of a heavy star literally making a hole in space.

::: steps
Iron core forms :: Fusion stops yielding energy; the core grows to about 1.4 solar masses.
Collapse :: Electrons are forced into protons; the core falls in under a second.
Bounce :: Matter hits nuclear density, rebounds, and a shockwave launches outward.
Stall and revival :: Neutrinos heat the stalled shock until it blasts through the star.
Brightening :: The star's outer layers are flung out and glow for weeks.
:::

## The thermonuclear kind

A [[Type Ia supernova]] has no massive star at all. It begins with a [[white dwarf]], the Earth-sized ember of a Sun-like star, drawing gas from a companion or merging with another dwarf. When it nears the 1.4 solar mass threshold, carbon ignites throughout its interior and the whole object is consumed in a thermonuclear runaway, leaving nothing behind. Because these explosions peak at nearly the same brightness, they work as standard candles. In 1998 two teams measuring distant Type Ia events found them dimmer than expected, evidence that the universe's expansion is accelerating, and the discovery won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics and gave us [[dark energy]] as a name for the cause.

## Where the elements come from

Almost every element heavier than hydrogen and helium was made in stars, and supernovas scatter the product. Oxygen, calcium, and iron are blown out of massive stars and Type Ia explosions, a process called [[nucleosynthesis]]. The same fires that give [[fireworks]] their reds and greens, strontium and barium, were forged in stellar interiors and exploded outward. Gold and other heaviest elements likely come mostly from neutron star collisions rather than supernovas, a point confirmed in 2017 with gravitational waves and light from one such merger.

Supernova shockwaves also accelerate [[cosmic ray|cosmic rays]] and trigger the collapse of nearby gas into new stars, which is how our Solar System may have begun. The [[Tycho Brahe|Tycho]] event of 1572 and Kepler's of 1604 are the last two seen in our own galaxy with the naked eye. We are overdue, and the red supergiant [[Betelgeuse]] is a popular candidate, though it is probably tens of thousands of years from exploding.

^ The Milky Way hosts about one supernova every 50 years, yet dust hides most of them. The last confirmed Galactic remnant is only a few centuries old.
