Hold a Rubik’s Cube and you hold 26 small pieces that look like one solid block until you turn a face. Each turn rotates one layer of nine pieces around its axis, and the six faces carry 54 coloured stickers between them. A few random turns leave it looking like a jumble, yet every jumble can be turned back into the solved state, with one colour on each face.

## The pieces

The 26 visible pieces come in three kinds: 8 corners with three colours each, 12 edges with two, and 6 centres with one. The centres are the exception, since each sits on a hidden core and never changes places with another centre. A turn only spins the centre of the face it belongs to, which is why the centres fix the colours of a solved cube. The usual scheme puts white opposite yellow, green opposite blue and red opposite orange, and it is drawn with white on top and green and red at the sides. Shown exploded, the pieces float apart in their places, and each turn is a [[permutation|Permutation]] that turns nine of them at once.

## Counting the arrangements

The cube has 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 reachable arrangements, about 43 quintillion, and the count comes from treating its pieces as a [[permutation|Permutation]] problem with twists attached. The eight corners can be placed in any order and each can be twisted three ways, but the last twist is forced once the other seven are set. The twelve edges follow the same pattern with flips, and one rule halves the result: the corner order and the edge order must have matching [[parity|Parity of a permutation]]. Multiplying these choices gives the total, and every arrangement in it can be reached by turns alone.
::: figures
40,320 :: ways to order the eight corners
2,187 :: twist patterns the corners can take
239,500,800 :: edge orders possible, given the corners
2,048 :: flip patterns the edges can take
:::

In 2010 Tomas Rokicki, Herbert Kociemba, Morley Davidson and John Dethridge showed that every position can be solved in 20 moves or fewer, counting a half turn as one move. Their result settled [[God’s number]], the most moves any scramble needs, and when only quarter turns count, the figure is 26, proved in 2014. The [[superflip|Superflip]], which flips every edge in place while leaving the corners solved, is one of the positions that needs the full 20. A brute-force search of every arrangement was out of reach, so the proof grouped positions that behave alike and searched the groups instead.
^ Checking one arrangement a second would take about 1.4 trillion years, roughly a hundred times the age of the universe.

## From puzzle to sport

[[Ernő Rubik]], an architecture lecturer in Budapest, built the first version in 1974 while working out how the parts of a moving three-dimensional object could stay joined. A hidden core holds the pieces together while each layer turns around it, so the cube can twist without falling apart. The American toy firm [[Ideal Toy Company]] took it abroad in 1980 as Rubik’s Cube, and in 1982 the first [[World Rubik’s Cube Championship]] was held in Budapest, where solvers competed for the world title.
^ Its first name was the Magic Cube, a literal translation of the Hungarian Bűvös kocka.

Most beginners solve it one layer at a time, with short sequences of turns that place each piece without disturbing those already set. Faster solvers memorise longer sequences and plan several pieces at once, and top [[speedcubers|Speedcubing]] now solve in under five seconds. Some go further and solve [[blindfolded|Blindfold cubing]], memorising the scramble before they begin. Mathematicians took a more systematic route, and [[Thistlethwaite’s algorithm]] solves any cube in at most 52 moves by stepping through a chain of smaller sets of positions, a method devised in the early 1980s.
