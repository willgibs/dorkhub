In 1974 a Hungarian architecture lecturer named [[Ernő Rubik|Ernő Rubik]] built a block of wood and rubber bands in his Budapest flat, twisted one face, and found he could not easily undo it. He had made a cube of 26 visible pieces that rotate around one another without falling apart, and he spent about a month solving his own scrambled creation. Nearly fifty years on, over 450 million have been sold, and the object sits somewhere between toy, puzzle and mathematics lesson. It is a three-by-three-by-three cube whose six faces each hold nine coloured stickers, and any face can turn a quarter, half or three-quarter revolution.

## What is inside

Take a cube apart and it is far simpler than it looks. There are 8 corner pieces with three coloured faces, 12 edge pieces with two, and 6 centre pieces with one, each fixed to a central core and never moving relative to the others. The centres therefore define the colour of each face, which is why the solved colour scheme is always the same up to mirror image, with white opposite yellow, green opposite blue and red opposite orange. The visible 26 pieces are held in place by interlocking tabs and a hidden spring-and-screw arrangement in modern cubes, not by Rubik's original rubber-and-wood pivots.

The pieces can be arranged in 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 ways, roughly 43 quintillion. The figure comes from counting corner and edge permutations and orientations, then dividing by 12 because three constraints bind them: corners twist in sets, edges flip in pairs, and a [[Permutation parity|parity]] rule links swaps of the two kinds. In practice this means that a cube reassembled at random from loose pieces has only a one-in-twelve chance of being solvable. Stacked end to end, that many cubes would stretch far beyond the nearest stars.

## From Budapest to the world

Rubik meant it as a teaching aid for three-dimensional thinking, and he patented the design in Hungary in 1975. Selling it abroad was harder in a planned economy, until a trade fair in 1979 led the [[Ideal Toy Corporation]] to license it. Renamed and shipped worldwide from 1980, it became the decade's defining craze, with a [[Speedcubing|speed-solving]] scene that began with a first world championship in Budapest in 1982. The winner solved it in just under 23 seconds. Today the average human record sits below 5 seconds, and the single-solve world record has been under 4 seconds since 2023, held by Max Park at 3.13.

Solvers do not search; they memorise sequences of turns, written in [[Singmaster notation]], which names each face by a letter such as R for right and U for up. David Singmaster published the first widely used solution in 1981. Fast solvers use methods that chain together dozens of such algorithms, and some solve while [[Blindfolded cubing|blindfolded]] after studying the cube for a few seconds.

## The mathematics underneath

Every move on the cube is a permutation, and the full set of moves forms a mathematical structure studied in [[Group theory]] known as the Rubik's Cube group. Its elements are the 43 quintillion positions, and combining two moves always yields another position, which is precisely what makes it a group. The same idea of permutation underlies the wiring of the [[Enigma machine]], whose rotors shuffled letters in patterns that Polish and British codebreakers analysed with the same algebra.

The cube raises the question of how many moves are enough. In 1981 Morwen Thistlethwaite devised a method that narrowed any position down through nested subgroups, guaranteeing a solution in at most 52 moves, and later work cut the bound gradually. In 2010 Tomas Rokicki, Herbert Kociemba, Morley Davidson and John Dethridge proved, using a donation of idle computer time from Google, that every position can be solved in 20 or fewer moves in the face-turn metric. That number is called [[God's number]], and a few special positions really do need all 20. The result shows how an enormous, yet finite, space can still be tightly bounded, a small taste of how [[Infinity]] differs from merely very large.
