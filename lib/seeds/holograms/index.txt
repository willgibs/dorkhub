Look at a hologram of a chess position, tilt your head, and the knight slides sideways to reveal the pawn behind it. Move closer and the board enlarges; focus your eyes past the glass and the far pieces sharpen while the near ones blur. Nothing is painted on the plate. Under a microscope a hologram is a gray, swirling smear, a fingerprint of light too fine for the eye to read, yet when lit correctly it rebuilds the wavefront that the original objects once sent out. You are not seeing a picture of the scene; you are receiving the light the scene would have delivered.

The trick is recording phase as well as brightness. A photograph stores only how bright light is at each point, which discards the information about distance and depth. A hologram splits a [[Laser|laser]] beam in two: one half bounces off the object onto a photographic plate, the other travels straight to the plate as a reference. Where the two meet they form an [[Interference (wave propagation)|interference]] pattern of fringes, spaced about a wavelength of light apart, roughly half a micrometre. Those fringes encode the phase of every ray, and the plate must stay still to a fraction of that distance during exposure, which is why early holographers worked on vibration-isolated tables, sometimes heavy slabs floating on inflated inner tubes.

^ Gabor was trying to sharpen the [[Electron microscope|electron microscope]], not to make 3D images. The picture was a side effect of a lens problem.

## Light as the thing recorded

[[Dennis Gabor]], a Hungarian engineer working at British Thomson-Houston in Rugby, conceived holography in 1947 while trying to correct lens aberrations in the electron microscope. He coined "hologram" from the Greek for "whole message" and demonstrated it with a mercury arc lamp, whose light was feeble and only partly coherent. The images were murky, overlapped by a twin ghost image, and the idea languished for fifteen years. Gabor received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1971.

The laser changed everything. In 1962 [[Emmett Leith]] and Juris Upatnieks at the University of Michigan used one with an off-axis reference beam, which separated the twin images and produced the first sharp three-dimensional holograms of objects, a toy train and a bird among them. Independently in the Soviet Union, [[Yuri Denisyuk]] recorded holograms in thick emulsions that could be viewed in ordinary white light, an approach connected to the colour method of [[Lippmann photography]] from 1891.

## Why a broken piece still shows everything

Every point on the plate received light from every point of the object, so the information is smeared across the whole surface rather than stored in one spot. Cut a hologram in half and each piece still reconstructs the entire scene, as though you were looking through a smaller window; cut it into scraps and you get the same view, just from a narrower vantage and with a grainier, noisier image. Resolution and the range of viewing angles shrink with the piece, but nothing is missing. The [[Speckle pattern|speckle]] that makes laser images look sandy is the visible cost of using coherent light.

Most holograms on credit cards and banknotes are [[Rainbow hologram|rainbow holograms]], invented by Stephen Benton in 1968, which sacrifice vertical parallax so they can be seen in ordinary light, shifting colour as you tilt them. They are stamped as relief patterns on foil, a type of [[Diffraction grating|diffraction grating]], by the million.

## Beyond pictures

The same physics stores data. [[Holographic data storage]] writes pages of bits as interference patterns throughout the volume of a crystal, promising enormous density, though commercial products have repeatedly stalled. Computers now calculate fringe patterns directly, and spatial light modulators shape laser beams into arrays that trap and move microscopic particles in [[Optical tweezers|optical tweezers]].

The idea has also migrated into theory. In the 1990s Gerard 't Hooft and Leonard Susskind proposed the [[Holographic principle]]: the information inside a region of space may be fully encoded on its boundary, a notion that grew from the entropy of [[Black holes|black holes]], which scales with surface area rather than volume. The name is an analogy, but a telling one.
