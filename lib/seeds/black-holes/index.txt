A black hole is what is left when matter is packed so tightly that the escape speed at its edge exceeds the speed of light. Nothing needs to be exotic about the material: the Sun would become one if squeezed to a sphere about 6 km across, and Earth would do it at about 18 mm, the size of a marble. The boundary of no return is the [[event horizon|Event horizon]], and its radius for a non-spinning hole is the [[Schwarzschild radius]], named for Karl Schwarzschild, who found the solution to Einstein's equations in 1915 while serving on the Russian front. The horizon is not a surface you could touch; it is a one-way membrane in spacetime, beyond which every possible path leads inward.

^ John Michell and Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined "dark stars" in the 1780s using Newtonian gravity. The idea of a star too heavy for light to leave is older than relativity.

## Where they come from

Most known black holes are the remains of stars. When a star of perhaps 20 or more solar masses exhausts its fuel, its core can no longer hold itself up and collapses in a fraction of a second, often accompanied by a [[supernova|Supernovas]]; if the core is heavy enough, no known force stops it. The first strong candidate was [[Cygnus X-1]], a bright X-ray source discovered in 1964 and tied in 1971 to a blue supergiant whirling around an unseen companion of roughly 20 solar masses, too heavy to be a neutron star. Stellar holes range from a few to a few dozen solar masses, and some are far larger than that.

At the other extreme sit the supermassive ones. At the center of the Milky Way, [[Sagittarius A*]] weighs about 4 million Suns; astronomers, including the groups of Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel, tracked stars orbiting it for decades, one passing at a few percent of the speed of light, and the work earned them a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics. In the galaxy M87 the hole is about 6.5 billion solar masses, with a horizon larger than our solar system's planetary orbits. How such giants grew so quickly in the early universe is still debated.

## Seeing the invisible

A black hole emits no light of its own, but gas spiralling toward it forms an [[accretion disk|Accretion disk]] heated to millions of degrees. Light from that disk follows paths bent by [[gravitational lensing|Gravitational lensing]]: rays from the far side of the disk are bent up and over the hole, so an observer sees the back of the disk arching above the shadow while the near side passes in front. Jean-Pierre Luminet computed this picture in 1979 on an IBM 7040, drawing it by hand in ink dots because the printouts were crude. The side of the disk rotating toward you looks brighter, because motion toward the observer beams the light forward, and a thin ring of light hugs the dark shadow, made of photons that circled the hole before escaping.

The real thing was imaged in April 2019, when the [[Event Horizon Telescope]], a network of radio dishes spanning Earth, released a picture of the M87 hole: a lopsided glowing ring around a dark center, matching predictions from relativity. In 2022 it did the same for Sagittarius A*. Another route is sound of a kind: in September 2015 the LIGO detectors recorded [[gravitational waves|Gravitational waves]] from two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses merging 1.3 billion light-years away, ending in a ringing of spacetime itself.

## What breaks down

Falling in is not uneventful. A small hole's tides would stretch an astronaut head to foot long before the horizon, a fate called [[spaghettification|Spaghettification]], while for a supermassive hole the crossing could be gentle, with the stretching coming later. A distant observer, meanwhile, sees the faller slow, redden and fade at the edge, since clocks run slow in deep gravity, which is why black holes are a favorite of [[time travel|Time travel]] speculation, though they offer no way back.

At the center, general relativity predicts a singularity, where density and curvature diverge toward [[infinity|Infinity]]; most physicists read this as the theory failing rather than nature truly producing an infinity. In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects make holes glow faintly with [[Hawking radiation|Hawking radiation]], so they slowly evaporate, raising the information paradox of what becomes of what fell in. One proposed escape, the holographic principle, holds that everything inside might be encoded on the horizon like a hologram, an idea explored through [[holograms|Holograms]]-style mathematics in string theory.
