At 4:17 in the afternoon, Eastern time, a spidery four-legged craft named [[Eagle|Apollo Lunar Module]] tilted toward the dark, flat plain of the [[Sea of Tranquility]], its descent engine kicking up dust across the ground. Inside, [[Neil Armstrong]] and [[Buzz Aldrin]] watched the altitude readouts while Mission Control debated whether a computer that kept complaining could be trusted. The final stretch would be flown by hand, toward a spot the crew chose themselves.

## Three men, one rocket

The [[Saturn V]] stood about 110 meters tall, and most of its launch mass was propellant. Engineers stacked the rocket in the Vehicle Assembly Building at [[Kennedy Space Center]] and crawled it to the pad. Atop the rocket sat the [[Columbia|Apollo command and service module]], which would carry the crew home, and the [[Eagle|Apollo Lunar Module]], which would carry two of them down. [[Michael Collins]] stayed in Columbia while [[Neil Armstrong]] and [[Buzz Aldrin]] moved into Eagle, a craft built for the Moon's vacuum and low gravity, and for nothing else. Its descent engine would brake the craft toward the surface, and a smaller engine on top would lift the crew back off.

## The computer and the boulders

The [[Apollo Guidance Computer]] had 2,048 words of erasable memory, its scratchpad for working numbers. Its program sat in a separate fixed memory of 36,864 words, built as [[Core rope memory]], where each bit depended on whether a wire ran through a ferrite ring or around it. Workers at Raytheon threaded those wires by hand, and the women who did the weaving were nicknamed the Little Old Ladies. The crew typed verb and noun codes into a keypad and display called the DSKY. The navigation had to fit inside that scratchpad, with almost no slack.

^ Those 2,048 words came to about 4 kilobytes, roughly one thousandth of a typical phone photo.

During the descent, the computer flashed the 1202 and 1201 alarms several minutes before touchdown. They meant the machine was overloaded, with more work arriving than its schedule could handle. It dropped lower-priority jobs and kept the guidance running. Flight controllers in Houston checked the numbers, saw that the lander was still on track, and told the crew to continue.

The planned site turned out to be strewn with boulders, so Armstrong took manual control and steered past them toward a flatter patch, watching the fuel gauge fall. Nobody on the ground could fly the craft, so the final call rested with the two men inside. Eagle touched down with only seconds of fuel to spare, and Armstrong reported the landing to Houston.

## What the Moon kept

The first walk on the surface lasted about two and a half hours, and the crew gathered rock and soil to bring home, about 21.5 kilograms in all. Some of it was fine gray [[dust|Lunar regolith]], which clung to the suits and got into the cabin. They unrolled a sheet of aluminum foil to catch particles streaming from the [[solar wind|Solar wind]], then rolled it up and carried it back. They also set down a panel of mirrors that still bounces laser pulses from Earth, a target for [[Lunar laser ranging]].

The footprints they left are still there, because the Moon has no wind or rain to wear them away. They could last for millions of years, unless a meteorite strikes the same spot. The rocks have fed decades of research, and laboratories still cut and measure them.
