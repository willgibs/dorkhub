On 16 July 1969, a [[Saturn V]] rose from Florida carrying Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins toward a goal set eight years earlier by President Kennedy: land a man on the Moon and return him safely before the decade ended. Four days later, the lunar module [[Lunar Module Eagle|Eagle]] touched down in the [[Mare Tranquillitatis|Sea of Tranquility]] with, by NASA's later estimate, around 25 seconds of descent fuel to spare. Armstrong stepped onto the surface at 02:56 UTC on 21 July, and an audience of roughly 600 million people watched grainy black-and-white pictures arrive from a quarter of a million miles away. Behind the spectacle lay the work of some 400,000 people and a program that had already buried three astronauts in the [[Apollo 1]] fire of January 1967.

The mission's architecture was a gamble made in 1962: [[Lunar orbit rendezvous]], in which a small two-stage lander would descend while the command module waited in orbit. It saved enormous weight compared with landing the whole spacecraft, but it meant that if Eagle's ascent engine failed to fire, Armstrong and Aldrin had no rescue. Collins, alone in the command module Columbia, spent 28 hours orbiting the Moon, and for part of each lap he was out of radio contact behind the far side, farther from every other human than anyone had ever been.

^ Collins later wrote that he felt not loneliness but awareness of life on the far side of the Moon, in the form of the two men below and everyone on Earth behind them.

## The computer and the alarms

The [[Apollo Guidance Computer]] carried about 2,048 words of erasable memory and 36,864 words of fixed memory, the latter woven by hand as copper wire threaded through magnetic cores, a technique that earned it the nickname "rope memory". It ran at roughly 1 MHz and was among the first computers built with integrated circuits. Its software, led at MIT by [[Margaret Hamilton (software engineer)|Margaret Hamilton]], was designed to shed low-priority work when overloaded rather than crash.

That design was tested at about 6,000 feet above the surface. As Eagle descended, the computer flashed a [[1202 alarm|1202 program alarm]], then a 1201, indicating it was running out of processing capacity. The cause was the rendezvous radar, which had been left switched on and was flooding the computer with needless counting work. Steve Bales, the guidance officer in Houston, and his support engineer Jack Garman judged the alarms survivable, and the computer kept dropping the unimportant tasks while the landing continued.

## Landing, walking, leaving

Beyond the alarms, Armstrong saw that the autopilot was steering Eagle toward a field of boulders beside a large crater. He took manual control and flew past it, skimming along looking for smooth ground while Aldrin read out altitude and speed. The first words from the surface were "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed." Over about two and a half hours outside, the two men planted a flag, set up a seismometer and a [[Laser ranging retroreflector]] that scientists still bounce lasers off today, and collected about 21.5 kilograms of [[Lunar regolith]].

The return was tense in smaller ways. During cabin preparations, a circuit-breaker switch needed to arm the ascent engine was found broken, apparently knocked off by a bulky backpack. Aldrin jammed a felt-tip pen into the gap, and it worked. The crew splashed down in the Pacific on 24 July and spent 21 days in quarantine at the [[Lunar Receiving Laboratory]], on the then-credible chance of lunar pathogens.

^ The 1969 television signal was received at the [[Parkes Observatory]] in Australia, whose dish had to be steered through a gale to catch the picture.

## What came back

The samples shifted ideas about the Moon's origin, showing it was ancient, dry, and chemically close to Earth's mantle, evidence that fed the giant-impact hypothesis. They also set the template for lunar science elsewhere: some of the Moon's rocks were later found on Earth itself, in meteorites such as [[Allan Hills 81005]], picked up on the ice of [[Antarctica]] in 1982 and recognized as lunar a year later, the first meteorite identified as coming from the Moon.
