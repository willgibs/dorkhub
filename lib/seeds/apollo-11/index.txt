On 16 July 1969, a [[Saturn V]] rose from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center carrying Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins. Four days later, two of them stood on the Moon while the third circled alone overhead. The mission fulfilled President Kennedy's 1961 pledge to land a man on the Moon and return him safely before the decade ended, and it did so with about five months to spare. An estimated 600 million people watched the television pictures, a record for the time.

The landing was nearly aborted more than once. While the lunar module descended, the [[Apollo Guidance Computer]], which had roughly 4 KB of erasable memory, began throwing program alarms. Mission Control's 26-year-old guidance officer Steve Bales judged them safe to ignore, and the [[1202 alarm]] became one of the most famous moments of the flight. The computer was being overloaded by data from a rendezvous radar left switched on, but its priority scheduler dropped low-value tasks and kept the landing program running.

^ The astronauts' mission patch showed an eagle carrying an olive branch, and the olive branch was added so the bird's talons would not look hostile.

## Landing with seconds to spare

Aldrin and Armstrong flew [[Lunar Module Eagle]] toward the [[Sea of Tranquility]], a smooth plain picked because photographs showed few obstacles. But the computer's guidance had carried them long, past the intended spot, and Armstrong saw the target ellipse filling with a crater ringed by boulders, each as large as a car. He took over manual control, flew horizontally over the rubble searching for flat ground, and kept descending slowly. Aldrin read out altitude and fuel figures throughout; Mission Control called "60 seconds" of fuel before touchdown, then "30 seconds."

Armstrong settled the craft down at 20:17 UTC on 20 July. Later analysis put the remaining fuel at roughly 25 seconds' worth to the point of no return. His heart rate during the descent, recorded by biomedical sensors, rose to about 150 beats per minute. The first words from the surface were "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."

## Two and a half hours outside

Armstrong stepped onto the surface first, followed by Aldrin about 19 minutes later. They planted a flag, collected about 21.5 kg of rock and soil, and set up a seismometer and a [[Laser ranging retroreflector]], a panel of mirrors that observatories still bounce lasers off to measure the distance to the Moon to within a few millimetres. The soil, [[Lunar regolith]], was fine and sharp, clung to boots and suits, and smelled to both men like spent gunpowder once tracked back inside. They also left a plaque, a small silicon disc carrying messages from world leaders, and a patch commemorating the fatal fire of [[Apollo 1]].

Meanwhile, [[Michael Collins (astronaut)]] orbited alone in the command module Columbia, out of radio contact for 48 minutes each lap as he passed behind the Moon. He later wrote that he felt not loneliness but awareness of life on the other side. Some of the television signal was received by the [[Parkes Observatory]] in Australia, whose dish picked up sharper pictures than the American stations during part of the moonwalk.

## Quarantine and legacy

The crew splashed down on 24 July and went into quarantine in a trailer, then into the [[Lunar Receiving Laboratory]] in Houston, in case the Moon harboured pathogens. None were found. The samples revealed a Moon of basalt and anorthosite rock, roughly 3.7 billion years old at the Tranquility site, and later returned samples helped establish the giant-impact theory of lunar origin.

Moon rocks have since turned up in unexpected places. Meteorite hunters in [[Antarctica]] found [[Allan Hills A81005]] in 1982, the first meteorite recognized as a [[Lunar meteorite]], showing that impacts had long been throwing pieces of the Moon to Earth for free.
