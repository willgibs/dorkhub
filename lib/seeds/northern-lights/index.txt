The northern lights, or aurora borealis, are a glow in the upper atmosphere produced when charged particles from space slam into air molecules. Seen from the ground, they arrive as faint grey arcs, then ripple into rayed curtains of green, sometimes edged with a violet-pink fringe below and a deep red crown above. The curtains can stretch across a whole sky yet are only a few hundred metres thick, and they shift on a timescale of seconds. Every one of them begins at the Sun, whose [[Solar wind|solar wind]] streams past Earth at roughly 400 kilometres per second and takes about two to four days to arrive.

Earth does not simply let that wind in. The planet's [[Magnets|magnetic field]] deflects most of it, but during disturbances the field is squeezed on the sunward side and stretched into a long tail on the night side, storing energy like a drawn bow. When the tail snaps back, electrons are accelerated down field lines toward the poles, and they strike the thin upper air in a ring around each magnetic pole called the [[Auroral oval]]. That is why Fairbanks, Tromsø and Yellowknife see displays most nights of a dark winter, while Rome sees them a few times in a century.

^ The same lights ring the south pole too: the [[Aurora australis|southern lights]], best seen from [[Antarctica]], flare at the same moment as their northern twin.

## One element, two colours

The green and the red are the same oxygen, glowing at different heights. An incoming electron kicks an oxygen atom into an excited state, and when the atom relaxes it releases a photon of a fixed wavelength, the same principle that makes the colours in [[Fireworks]] and the lines in any [[Emission spectrum|emission spectrum]]. Green, at 557.7 nanometres, comes mainly from roughly 100 to 300 kilometres up, where the air is dense enough for the excited atoms to radiate within about three-quarters of a second. Red, at 630 nanometres, comes from above about 300 kilometres.

::: steps
Arrival :: Electron strikes an oxygen atom, lifting it to an excited state.
Low altitude :: Below ~300 km, collisions quickly quench the slow red state.
Green :: Atom emits at 557.7 nm after about 0.7 seconds.
High altitude :: Above ~300 km, air is thin enough to wait out the delay.
Red :: Atom emits at 630 nm after roughly 100 seconds.
:::

The difference is a matter of patience. Both are "[[Forbidden transition|forbidden]]" transitions, meaning the excited atom is slow to let go: about 0.7 seconds for green but nearly two minutes for red. In the denser air below about 300 kilometres, a collision almost always steals the energy before the red photon can form, so red survives only where the atmosphere is thin enough to leave the atom alone. Blue and violet, from ionised nitrogen molecules, appear at the lower fringe, and a purple hem is common in strong storms.

^ Red oxygen emission was misidentified for decades; the green line was unmatched with any known element until 1925, and was briefly credited to a hypothetical "geocoronium".

## Storms and history

Activity follows the Sun's roughly 11-year cycle, peaking a little after solar maximum, when coronal mass ejections hurl billions of tonnes of plasma at Earth and trigger a [[Geomagnetic storm]]. In the great storm of 1–2 September 1859, the [[Carrington Event]], aurorae were reported as far south as Cuba and Hawaii, and telegraph operators received shocks and sent messages on induced current alone with their batteries disconnected. In May 2024 the strongest storm in two decades pushed vivid colour to Florida, Texas and southern Europe.

The scientific story was long unclear. Galileo's contemporary Pierre Gassendi named the phenomenon aurora borealis in 1621, after the Roman dawn goddess and the north wind. It was the Norwegian [[Kristian Birkeland]] who, between 1896 and 1913, shot electrons at a magnetised sphere in a vacuum chamber and reproduced glowing rings at its poles, arguing correctly that particles from the Sun cause aurorae, though most of his peers rejected the idea for years. Today satellites have found more, including a thin mauve ribbon discovered by amateur photographers and named [[Steve (atmospheric phenomenon)|STEVE]], and other planets with fields, such as [[Jupiter's aurorae|Jupiter]], have their own, thousands of times more powerful.
