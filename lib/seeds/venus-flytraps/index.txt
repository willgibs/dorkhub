Venus flytraps (*[[Dionaea muscipula]]*) grow wild in only one corner of the world: a strip of damp, sandy, acidic pine savanna within about 100 kilometres of Wilmington, North Carolina, reaching just into South Carolina. Each plant is a low rosette of leaves whose tips are hinged into twin lobes, edged with stiff bristles called cilia and, when open, often blushing red inside from [[Anthocyanin]] pigment. The soil is hopeless for a plant: waterlogged, starved of nitrogen and phosphorus. So the flytrap, like every [[Carnivorous plant]], buys what the ground cannot supply by catching animals, mostly beetles, spiders and ants rather than the flies of its name. Darwin called it "one of the most wonderful in the world."

## A trap that counts

Inside each lobe sit three slender trigger hairs. Bending one fires an electrical signal, but a single touch does nothing, because rain and falling debris touch leaves constantly. If a second touch arrives within roughly 20 seconds, the trap snaps shut. The plant is doing arithmetic on a leaf with no nervous system, and the 2016 study led by Rainer Hedrich's group at the University of Würzburg (Current Biology) showed how: each touch triggers an [[Action potential]], and each also lets calcium flow into the cells in a measured dose. The first touch raises calcium a little, and the second, arriving before it fades, pushes it over the threshold for closing.

The lobes shut in about a tenth of a second, but at first only into a loose cage: the cilia interlock like fingers, and small insects can slip out through the gaps, saving the plant the cost of digesting a crumb. A struggling captive keeps touching the hairs. After a total of five touches the trap seals tightly and the digestive glands begin to secrete. Hedrich's team found that the counting continues past the closing: the more the prey moves, the more jasmonate hormone signalling the plant makes, and the more enzymes it pumps out.

::: steps
Touch 1 :: A trigger hair bends; an action potential fires and calcium rises slightly.
Touch 2 :: Within about 20 seconds, calcium crosses the threshold and the trap closes loosely.
Touches 3-4 :: Struggling prey keeps stimulating hairs; the lobes press tighter.
Touch 5 :: Trap seals airtight; glands flood with digestive fluid.
Days later :: Nutrients absorbed; trap reopens, leaving the husk of the prey.
:::

^ The electrical signals travel across the leaf at roughly 10 centimetres per second, a slow, plant-scale cousin of [[Lightning]] in the sense that both are discharges, only this one carries arithmetic.

## How the snap works

The closing is not muscle. Before firing, the open lobe is held in a bowed, convex shape under tension, like a bent plastic lid. The action potentials trigger a rapid change in the cell walls and water movement, and the lobe flips to concave, as a spring-loaded toy snaps inside out. Research reported by Lakshminarayanan Mahadevan's group at Harvard in 2005 modelled this as a geometric instability rather than simple growth. Once sealed, digestion takes about five to twelve days, with enzymes and acid breaking prey down so the plant can absorb nitrogen and phosphorus. Each trap can digest only a handful of meals before it blackens and dies, and the plant grows new ones.

The flytrap even manages its own colour. The red insides are thought to help lure prey, though that is debated, and the nectar-like secretions along the rim are an additional bait.

## Living with fire, and a surprise field

In the wild the plant depends on [[Wildfire ecology]]: the [[Longleaf pine]] savanna burns every few years, clearing the shrubs that would otherwise shade the low rosettes out. Without fire, populations decline, and poaching has pushed the species toward the threatened category. Its wild range sits around the [[Green Swamp]] and similar [[Bog]] edges, and a close cousin in sticky ambush, the [[Sundew]], shares some of its habitat.

In 2020, a Mainz team led by Anke Fabricant used a sensitive atomic magnetometer, the same kind of tool as in [[Magnetocardiography]], to detect the tiny [[Magnets]]-style field produced when a trap fires: about 0.5 picotesla, thousands of times weaker than Earth's field. It was the first such recording from a plant's action potential, and a reminder that even a bog weed produces measurable physics.

^ The measured field was roughly a hundred million times weaker than a fridge magnet's, yet the same instrument class reads human heartbeats.
