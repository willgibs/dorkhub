The *Mona Lisa* is a half-length portrait, barely bigger than a large laptop screen, painted in oil on a single plank of poplar. It shows a woman seated before an invented landscape of winding paths, a bridge and receding blue mountains, her hands folded, her gaze turned toward the viewer with the faintest suggestion of a smile. [[Leonardo da Vinci]] began it in Florence around 1503 and was still working on it years later; he never delivered it to the sitter's family and carried it with him to France, where it stayed. Today it hangs behind bulletproof glass in the [[Louvre]], seen by something like ten million visitors a year, most of whom spend under a minute in front of it.

## A portrait that stayed with its painter

The sitter is generally identified as [[Lisa Gherardini]], wife of the Florentine cloth and silk merchant [[Francesco del Giocondo]], which is why the French call the painting *La Joconde* and the Italians *La Gioconda*. The identification rests on a 1503 marginal note by a Florence official, Agostino Vespucci, in a printed book, which records Leonardo working on a portrait of "Lisa del Giocondo". Leonardo left for Milan and later Rome, took the panel with him, and kept refining it. He moved to France in 1516 at the invitation of [[Francis I of France]], and the painting entered the royal collection, probably after his death in 1519.

The painting's famous softness comes from [[Sfumato]], the building of transitions from light to shadow through many ultra-thin glazes, so that no hard outline separates lip from cheek. The corners of the mouth dissolve into shadow, which is why the smile seems to flicker depending on where your eye lands: look at the eyes and the mouth appears to curve, look at the mouth and it flattens. Behind her, [[Aerial perspective]] turns distant rock steadily bluer and paler, a method Leonardo studied in the atmosphere itself.

^ The panel is poplar, which warps with humidity; a long crack running down from the top of the head has been stabilised since the 1950s, and the Louvre keeps the painting in a climate-controlled case.

## The burglary that made a legend

For centuries the painting was admired by artists and connoisseurs but was not a household image; works by Raphael were often rated higher. On the morning of 21 August 1911, [[Vincenzo Peruggia]], an Italian glazier who had worked on the Louvre's protective cases, walked out with it under his smock, having hidden in a storeroom overnight. The [[Theft of the Mona Lisa]] went unnoticed for hours, and when the museum closed for a week and the news broke, newspapers across the world ran the empty wall on their front pages. Crowds queued to stare at the blank space where it had hung. Pablo Picasso and the poet Guillaume Apollinaire were briefly questioned by police.

::: timeline
c. 1503 :: Leonardo begins the portrait in Florence
1516 :: Leonardo takes it to France under Francis I
1797 :: Hung permanently in the Louvre

1913 :: Recovered in Florence after Peruggia tries to sell it
1963 :: Tours the United States, drawing huge crowds
:::

Peruggia kept the painting for over two years in a trunk in his Paris room, then tried to sell it to an art dealer in Florence in December 1913, where he was caught. He claimed patriotic motives, wanting to return it to Italy, and served under a year in prison. The painting toured Italy before returning to Paris, now a global celebrity.

## Afterlife as an image

Fame invited mockery. In 1919 Marcel Duchamp drew a moustache and goatee on a postcard reproduction and titled it [[L.H.O.O.Q.]], a pun that sounds like a crude French phrase, a gesture associated with [[Dada]]. Andy Warhol, Fernando Botero and countless advertisers followed. The painting itself has been attacked: acid thrown in 1956 and a rock hurled the same year damaged its lower section, which is why it now sits behind glass.

Its surface has aged into a yellowish haze, partly from darkened varnish and fine [[Craquelure]], so Leonardo's original cooler palette would have looked fresher. A rival version, the [[Isleworth Mona Lisa]], shows a younger-looking sitter in a similar pose; supporters think it is an earlier Leonardo, but most scholars are unconvinced.
