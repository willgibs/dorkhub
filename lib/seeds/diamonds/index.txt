A diamond is a crystal of pure carbon in which every atom is bonded to four neighbours in a rigid cubic lattice. That geometry makes it the hardest natural material and an unmatched conductor of heat, and it bends light so sharply that a well-cut stone throws it back as fire. The same element, rearranged into flat sheets, is soft [[Graphite|graphite]]; the difference between pencil lead and a gemstone is only how the atoms are stacked. Nearly all natural diamonds are between 1 and 3.5 billion years old, which means the stone on a finger is typically far older than the first animals, and often older than the oxygen in the air.

^ A diamond is not "forever" in air: it can burn at roughly 700–900 °C, and left in a vacuum it slowly converts toward graphite.

## Made deep, delivered fast

Diamonds form in the [[Mantle (geology)|Earth's mantle]] at depths of roughly 150 to 200 kilometres, at pressures above 45,000 atmospheres and temperatures above 900 °C. The right conditions exist beneath ancient, thick, cold continental cores called [[Craton|cratons]], whose deep keels stay stable for billions of years. Carbon there comes from ancient mantle material, and in some cases from subducted crust, and slowly crystallizes from metal-rich fluids. Inclusions trapped inside stones, tiny flecks of other minerals sealed in like insects in amber, let geologists date them and reconstruct the chemistry of places no drill will ever reach. Some stones from the deepest sources even hold [[Ringwoodite|ringwoodite]] containing water, evidence that the mantle holds far more of it than once assumed.

Diamonds only reach us because of a rare kind of eruption. A [[Kimberlite|kimberlite]] magma, rich in carbon dioxide and water, rises from the mantle at speeds estimated at tens of kilometres per hour, fast enough to carry dense rock and crystals upward before the diamonds can revert to graphite. The eruption blasts through the crust and leaves a carrot-shaped pipe, narrowing downward for kilometres. The pipe's soil is where miners dig, and kimberlite is named for Kimberley in South Africa, whose [[Kimberley Big Hole|Big Hole]] was excavated almost entirely by hand in the 1870s and after, reaching about 215 metres deep before it was abandoned. These are among the deepest-sourced eruptions known, and no kimberlite has erupted in human history. The [[Volcanoes|volcanoes]] that deliver diamonds belong to a vanished kind.

## Cut, counted and contested

The [[Cullinan Diamond]], found in 1905 at the Premier Mine near Pretoria, weighed 3,106 carats (about 621 grams), the largest gem-quality rough diamond ever recovered. It was cleaved into nine major stones and about a hundred smaller ones, and the two largest, Cullinan I and II, sit in British royal regalia. A carat is exactly 200 milligrams, a unit descended from carob seeds once used as counterweights on merchant scales. Colour comes from impurities: nitrogen turns stones yellow, while boron makes the rare blue ones.

Diamonds are also tied to violence. In civil wars in Sierra Leone, Angola and elsewhere, gems financed armies, producing the [[Blood diamond|"blood diamond"]] trade and, in 2003, the Kimberley Process certification scheme, whose definition of conflict stones critics consider narrow. Meanwhile the market's most disruptive change is chemical: since the 1950s, labs have grown real diamonds under high pressure, and [[Chemical vapor deposition|chemical vapor deposition]] now builds them atom by atom from methane. Lab-grown stones are physically and chemically diamond, distinguishable only by instruments. Most industrial diamond, used for cutting, drilling and grinding, is already synthetic.

## Beyond the jewellery case

Hardness is not toughness: a diamond resists scratching better than anything natural, yet a hammer blow along a cleavage plane can split it, which is exactly how cutters have long worked. On the [[Mohs scale of mineral hardness|Mohs scale]] it is a 10, though in absolute terms it is far harder than the 9 held by corundum. Scientists compress samples between two diamond tips in a [[Diamond anvil cell|diamond anvil cell]] to recreate pressures near Earth's core, and such experiments have even been used to hunt [[Lonsdaleite|lonsdaleite]], a rarer hexagonal form of carbon found in meteorite impact sites.
