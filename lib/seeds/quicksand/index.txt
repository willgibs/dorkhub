Quicksand is not a special kind of sand. It is ordinary sand, or silty sand, caught in a moment when water has pushed its grains apart and left them floating. The result is a soupy slurry that looks like firm ground, often at a river bank, a tidal flat, a lake shore or a marsh. A person steps on the crust, the crust gives, and a leg disappears to the knee. Films have taught generations to expect a slow, inevitable burial, but the reality is stranger: a trap that is easy to enter, almost impossible to leave by brute force, and nearly useless at drowning anyone who stays calm.

Sand grains normally rest against one another, each carrying part of the load, which is why a beach holds your weight. Quicksand forms when water flows upward through the sand, from a spring, an artesian seep or a rising tide, fast enough to lift the grains until they no longer touch. This is the idea of [[Pore water pressure|pore water pressure]]: the water carries the load instead of the grains, and the mass loses nearly all its strength. A shock can do the same thing, since vibration from footsteps, or from an earthquake in the larger-scale version called [[Liquefaction (soil)|liquefaction]], can briefly shake grains apart. Around Christchurch, New Zealand, the [[Christchurch earthquakes of 2010 and 2011|earthquakes of 2010 and 2011]] pushed silty water-laden sand up through the ground in thousands of [[Sand boil|sand boils]], burying streets in grey silt.

^ Because the water has to keep flowing upward, many quicksand patches are permanent features of a stream bed, while others vanish within minutes when the water pressure drops.

## Why you can't sink and can't leave

Quicksand is denser than a human body, roughly 2 grams per cubic centimetre against about 1 for a person, so you float in it much as you do in the Dead Sea. A 2005 study led by Daniel Bonn at the Ecole Normale Superieure in Paris, published in Nature, confirmed that no one can be swallowed whole; a person sinks to about the waist, and breathing makes the body rise slightly higher. The danger lies elsewhere. Quicksand is [[Thixotropy|thixotropic]], a property it shares with ketchup and some paints: it flows when disturbed and stiffens when left alone. Struggling liquefies the sand around you, and then, once you stop, the grains settle back and pack tight.

The trap closes when you try to pull a leg out. As the leg rises, it leaves a void, and water has not had time to flow in and refill it, so the pressure below the foot drops and the sand clamps. Bonn's group calculated that extracting a foot at a speed of one centimetre per second needs a force comparable to lifting a medium-sized car, around a ton. No human can do this, and yanking harder only makes it worse. The sensible escape is slow: wiggle the leg to let water seep back down to loosen the packing, lean back to spread your weight, and pull the foot out over many minutes.

::: guess
Question :: Can you drown in quicksand without any tide or rising water?
Answer :: Almost never. The usual killers are exposure, thirst and the incoming tide.
Why :: The sand is too dense to swallow you, but it can hold you in place for hours.
:::

## The tidal killers

Real deaths from quicksand come mostly from being stuck while the sea returns. [[Morecambe Bay]] in northwest England has shifting channels and patches of soft sand across 300 square kilometres of tidal flat, and its tide can come in faster than a person can walk. On 5 February 2004, 21 Chinese cockle pickers were drowned there, caught far from shore as the water rose around them. The [[Bay of Mont-Saint-Michel]] in Normandy, with one of Europe's greatest tidal ranges, has long been described with its [[Tidal bore|tidal bore]] and soft sands as treacherous, and rescues of walkers stuck in mud there are regular events.

Sand can also show itself as stranger things. A mixture of grains and water in the right proportion behaves like [[Oobleck]], a [[Non-Newtonian fluid|non-Newtonian fluid]] that is runny when slowly poured and rigid when struck. [[Quick clay]], found in Scandinavia and Canada, goes further, collapsing from solid to liquid when disturbed and swallowing whole hillsides. And where [[Lightning]] strikes dry sand, the heat melts the grains into branching glass tubes called [[Fulgurite|fulgurites]], which show how dramatically sand's character changes with the energy put into it.

^ Quicksand can be recognised by its surface alone only sometimes: a damp, rippling or slightly shiny patch in otherwise dry ground is the warning.
