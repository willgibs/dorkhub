Every night, in cycles of roughly ninety minutes, your brain stops behaving like a sleeping organ and starts behaving like a waking one. Your eyes dart beneath closed lids, your heart rate and breathing turn ragged, and your cortex fires in patterns resembling alertness, while your voluntary muscles are switched off. In that state you walk through cities that are also your childhood school, talk to people who are also someone else, and accept all of it as real. Everyone dreams, including people who swear they never do; they simply don't remember. Across a lifetime, a person spends something like six years dreaming.

Most vivid dreaming happens during [[REM sleep]], first identified in 1953 when graduate student [[Eugene Aserinsky]] and his supervisor Nathaniel Kleitman at the University of Chicago noticed bursts of rapid eye movement in sleeping subjects. Woken during those bursts, people reported dreams far more often than when woken at other times. Dreams are not exclusive to REM, however: people roused from non-REM sleep report mental experiences too, though they tend to be shorter, blander and more thoughtlike.

^ Dreams in REM are so reliably vivid that in 1953 Aserinsky first tested the finding on his own young son, wiring him up for sleep.

## The shape of a night

Sleep proceeds in cycles, and the REM portion of each cycle grows as the night goes on. The first REM period may last only five to ten minutes, while the last, shortly before you wake, can stretch past forty-five minutes or even an hour. That is why the longest and most elaborate dream of the night tends to arrive just before morning, and why you so often wake from one with the details still fresh. Cutting a night short by a couple of hours therefore costs you a disproportionate share of your dream time.

::: figures
90 min :: typical length of one sleep cycle
4 to 6 :: cycles in a night
20 to 25% :: share of adult sleep spent in REM
45+ min :: possible length of the final REM period
:::

During REM, neurons in the [[Pons]] trigger a muscle shutdown so complete that you cannot act out your dreams. When this brake fails, as in [[REM sleep behavior disorder]], sleepers punch, shout and leap from bed; Michel Jouvet's cats, whose pontine brake was surgically damaged in the 1960s, famously stalked and pounced on imaginary prey in their sleep. When the opposite happens and you wake while the paralysis lingers, you get [[Sleep paralysis]], often with a sensed presence in the room. In [[Narcolepsy]], the brain can slip into REM within minutes of falling asleep.

## Why we dream

No explanation has won. The [[Activation-synthesis hypothesis]], proposed by Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley in 1977, held that dreams are the cortex's attempt to make a story out of random brainstem signals. Sigmund Freud, in [[The Interpretation of Dreams]] (1899), had argued instead that dreams disguise forbidden wishes, a view that experiments have never supported. Other ideas link sleep to [[Memory consolidation]], with replay of the day's experience strengthening some memories and pruning others, or to rehearsal: the [[Threat simulation theory]] of Antti Revonsuo suggests dreams practice responses to danger, which would explain why a [[Nightmare]] feels so purposeful.

^ Mammals and birds show REM sleep, and [[Octopuses]] cycle between quiet sleep and a skin-flickering active phase that resembles it, hinting that the pattern may have evolved more than once.

The strangest dream state is [[Lucid dreaming]], in which the sleeper knows they are dreaming. In 1975 British psychophysiologist Keith Hearne and later Stephen LaBerge at Stanford showed it objectively: trained dreamers signalled from inside a dream with prearranged eye movements, which the recording equipment picked up. That result proved the dreaming mind can be reached from outside, and it keeps the question open of what dreams are for.
