In 1908, a chemist at Tokyo Imperial University sat down to a bowl of soup and wondered why it tasted so good. The soup was made with a sheet of dried seaweed, and the flavor it carried was not sweet, sour, salty or bitter, the four tastes Western science had recognized since antiquity. [[Kikunae Ikeda]] boiled down roughly 38 kilograms of [[Kombu]] to extract the responsible substance, and isolated crystals of glutamic acid. He named the sensation *umami*, from the Japanese *umai* (delicious) and *mi* (taste), and within a year had patented a seasoning that delivered it on demand.

## What it is

Umami is the savory, mouth-filling taste of broths, aged cheese, ripe tomatoes, cured ham and soy sauce. Its trigger is glutamate, an amino acid that is abundant in proteins but tastes of little while locked inside them. Fermenting, curing, ripening and long cooking snip proteins into free amino acids, which is why a young cheese is mild and a long-aged one is intensely savory. [[Parmigiano-Reggiano]], matured for at least 12 months, is among the richest natural sources, and a tomato's glutamate climbs steeply as it ripens.

Ikeda's seasoning, [[Monosodium glutamate]], was commercialized by the company that became Ajinomoto, whose name means "essence of taste". Glutamate is the same molecule whether it comes from kelp, cheese or a factory fermentation vat; the industrial product is made by fermenting sugar with bacteria, much as soy sauce or yogurt is made.
^ Human [[Breast milk]] is rich in free glutamate, around ten times the level in cow's milk, so most people first meet umami at their mother's breast.

## The doubling effect

Japanese cooks had long combined ingredients without knowing why it worked. In 1913, Ikeda's student Shintaro Kodama identified [[Inosinate]] as the savory compound in [[Katsuobushi]], the smoked, fermented bonito flakes used in [[Dashi]]. In 1957, Akira Kuninaka found [[Guanylate]] in dried shiitake mushrooms. Neither compound tastes strong alone, but combined with glutamate they multiply its effect: a mixture can taste several times more intense than the sum of its parts.

This explains pairings that seem to spring from instinct: kombu with bonito in dashi, Parmesan on a tomato sauce, bacon with eggs, anchovies on pizza. Tomato and cheese supply glutamate, meat or fish supplies inosinate, and each pair amplifies the other. The ancient Romans reached a similar result with [[Garum]], a fermented fish sauce that was effectively liquid umami, and modern [[Fish sauce]] is its direct descendant.

## Fighting for recognition

For most of the twentieth century, Western scientists treated umami as a Japanese curiosity, perhaps a mere texture or blend of the other tastes. A [[Taste receptor]] would settle the question. In 2000, Nirupa Chaudhari and colleagues at the University of Miami reported a glutamate-sensitive receptor in rat taste buds, and in 2002 Charles Zuker's group at the University of California, San Diego, described the heterodimer T1R1/T1R3, which responds to amino acids in humans. Umami was soon accepted as a basic taste, and later work has found glutamate receptors in the stomach and gut, suggesting the sensation helps the body signal that protein has arrived.

Meanwhile, umami's best-known seasoning acquired a reputation problem. A 1968 letter to the New England Journal of Medicine described numbness and palpitations after Chinese restaurant meals, launching what became known as [[Chinese restaurant syndrome]]. Blinded, placebo-controlled studies have repeatedly failed to reproduce the symptoms in ordinary doses, and regulators including the US Food and Drug Administration classify MSG as generally recognized as safe. The charge, scientists now widely agree, owed more to suspicion of an unfamiliar cuisine than to chemistry.
