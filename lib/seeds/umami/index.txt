Umami is the savoury taste of broth, aged cheese, ripe tomatoes and soy sauce, and the fifth basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter. Its chief trigger is glutamate, an amino acid freed as proteins break down during ripening, ageing and fermentation; two nucleotides, inosinate and guanylate, multiply its effect. The word was coined by the Japanese chemist [[Kikunae Ikeda]] from umai, delicious, and mi, taste.

^ Glutamate makes up more than half the free amino acids in human breast milk, at roughly 40 times its level in the mother's blood.

## A taste found in kelp broth

Ikeda, a professor at Tokyo Imperial University, had noticed a taste in dashi, the stock made with [[kombu|Kombu]] kelp, that none of the four classic tastes described, and he found it again in asparagus, tomatoes, cheese and meat. By 1908 he had drawn crystals of glutamic acid out of the kelp and shown that they carried it. That year he patented a seasoning based on its sodium salt, [[monosodium glutamate|Monosodium glutamate]], hoping cheap flavour would make plain, nourishing food more appealing and improve Japanese nutrition.

## Older than its name

Cooks had pursued the taste for millennia, mostly through fermentation, which splits proteins into free amino acids. Romans seasoned with [[garum|Garum]], fish and salt left to ferment for weeks or months. In Pompeii, [[Aulus Umbricius Scaurus]] grew rich on it: his name or his workers' appear on almost a third of the fish-sauce jars found there and at Herculaneum, and mosaic jars in his atrium floor advertised his sauces. In Japan the work fell largely to [[Aspergillus oryzae]], the mould that turns soybeans and grain into soy sauce and miso, and classic dashi pairs kombu with [[katsuobushi|Katsuobushi]], skipjack tuna smoked and mould-cured until it resembles a block of wood.

## The powder and the panic

Commercial MSG was first made by breaking down wheat gluten with acid. Today most of it is brewed by bacteria, chiefly [[Corynebacterium glutamicum]], a microbe isolated in the 1950s, reportedly from soil collected at Tokyo's Ueno Zoo; fed sugar, it yields more than two million tonnes of glutamate a year. In 1968 a letter to the New England Journal of Medicine described numbness and palpitations after meals in Chinese restaurants and named MSG among possible causes. Printed under the heading "[[Chinese-Restaurant Syndrome|Chinese restaurant syndrome]]", it fixed suspicion on one cuisine, although parmesan and tomato sauce are rich in the same free glutamate.

::: myth
Myth :: MSG commonly causes headaches, flushing and palpitations
Actually :: Double-blind trials have not reliably reproduced such reactions at normal doses
:::

## A receptor that snaps shut

The tongue senses umami mainly through a receptor made of two proteins, T1R1 and T1R3. Glutamate binds near the hinge of a [[Venus flytrap domain]] on T1R1, a two-lobed clamp that closes around it, and inosinate or guanylate can lodge beside it to hold the trap shut. That probably explains why the two kinds of molecule taste far stronger together than apart, the effect dashi exploits. For decades Western researchers called glutamate a flavour enhancer rather than a taste, partly because alone it tastes faint; the receptor work largely settled the argument. Related sensations are still debated, among them [[kokumi|Kokumi]], a sense of richness and lingering fullness linked to the calcium-sensing receptor.

Umami seems to work as a protein detector, and animals whose diets changed have sometimes lost it. In the giant panda, a bear that turned to bamboo, the gene for T1R1 survives only as a [[pseudogene|Pseudogene]], disabled by one estimate about four million years ago. Sea lions and dolphins, which swallow fish whole, have lost a working copy too. Birds generally lack the sweet receptor, and [[hummingbirds|Hummingbird]] got round that by rebuilding their umami receptor to detect sugar, a switch that may have helped them diversify into more than 300 species.

^ A cat's umami receptor ignores glutamate entirely; it responds to nucleotides instead, which may help explain the feline weakness for tuna.
