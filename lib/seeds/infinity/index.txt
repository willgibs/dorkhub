Infinity is not a number you can reach by counting, but a way of describing things that never run out: the whole numbers 1, 2, 3 and onward, the points on a line, the steps of a division that is halved again and again. For most of history it was treated as a warning sign rather than an object. Aristotle allowed only a "potential" infinity, a process that could always continue, and rejected a completed one. The symbol ∞ appeared in print in 1655, in John Wallis's work on conic sections, and the sense that infinity could be handled as a finished thing, with arithmetic of its own, arrived only in the 1870s.

The shift came from [[Georg Cantor]], a mathematician at Halle, who asked a childishly simple question: what does it mean for two collections to be the same size? His answer needed no counting, only pairing. If every item in one set can be matched with exactly one item in another, with none left over, the sets are the same size. Applied to infinite sets, this produces results that feel like cheating. The even numbers can be paired with all whole numbers (2 with 1, 4 with 2, 6 with 3), so a part of the counting numbers is as large as the whole.

^ [[Zeno's paradoxes]] dramatized the problem some 2,400 years ago: infinitely many ever-shorter steps seem to forbid reaching a goal, yet runners finish races.

## The hotel with no vacancies

[[Hilbert's Hotel]], a thought experiment popularized by David Hilbert in the 1920s, makes pairing vivid. A hotel with infinitely many rooms is full, yet a new guest can be housed by moving everyone from room n to room n+1. Even an infinite busload of newcomers fits: move each guest from room n to room 2n, freeing every odd-numbered room. The fractions, surprisingly, also fit, since they can be arranged in a grid and walked through in a zigzag so that each gets a room number. Every one of these sets has the same size, which Cantor named ℵ₀, [[Aleph numbers|aleph-null]].

## The diagonal

The surprise is that some sets will not fit. In 1891 Cantor published [[Cantor's diagonal argument]], and it needs only a pencil. Suppose someone claims to have a list, numbered 1, 2, 3 and running on forever, of every real number between 0 and 1, each written as a string of decimal digits. Build a new number by taking the first digit of the first entry, the second digit of the second, the third of the third, and so on down the diagonal, then change each of those digits to something else, say by adding one (turning 9 into 0). The new number differs from the first entry in its first digit, from the second in its second digit, and from every entry somewhere. So it is not on the list, and the list was not complete.

::: steps
List :: Assume every real between 0 and 1 sits in a numbered list.
Diagonal :: Read the nth digit of the nth number, for every n.
Change :: Replace each diagonal digit with a different one, such as adding one.
Compare :: The new number differs from entry n in digit n, so matches no row.
Conclude :: No list can hold all the reals, so they outnumber the counting numbers.
:::

The same trick works against any list, so the real numbers form a strictly larger infinity than the whole numbers, of size 2^ℵ₀. Cantor went on to show that taking all subsets of any set always yields a bigger set, so the sizes climb forever, a ladder of [[Transfinite numbers]] with no top. His next question, whether any infinity sits between ℵ₀ and the reals, is the [[Continuum hypothesis]]. [[Kurt Gödel]] showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved from standard set theory, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963 that it cannot be proved either.

^ Cantor's work was attacked by Leopold Kronecker, who called him a "corrupter of youth"; Cantor suffered recurring depression, though historians dispute how far the controversy caused it.

## Infinity at work, and infinity in trouble

Infinity is the engine of [[Calculus]], where Newton and Leibniz in the 1600s summed infinitely many infinitely thin slices, and where limits later gave the idea rigorous footing in the 1800s. Artists and geometers use it too: in [[Projective geometry]], parallel lines meet at a point at infinity, which is what a vanishing point in a Renaissance painting is. Its unrestricted use has costs, though. [[Russell's paradox]] of 1901, the set of all sets that do not contain themselves, showed naive set theory could contradict itself, and led to the careful axioms used today.

In physics, a real infinity usually signals that a theory has been pushed past its limits, as with the [[Singularity (gravitational)|Gravitational singularity]] predicted at the center of a black hole, where density formally diverges. Finite things can be astronomically big without becoming infinite: the [[Rubik's Cube]] has about 4.3 × 10^19 positions, yet any can be solved in at most 20 moves. Whether physical space, or time, is truly infinite remains unknown.
