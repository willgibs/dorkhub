Picture a boomerang and most people see a flat wooden stick spinning across a field and curling back toward the thrower's feet. That picture describes only one family of designs. Most boomerangs were never meant to come back: they were thrown at birds, game or enemies, and they dropped where they landed.

## Sticks built to hit

Throwing sticks that do not return were made in ancient Egypt and in parts of Africa as well as in Australia. The [[Tomb of Nebamun]], a painted tomb at Thebes from about 1350 BC, shows a hunter in a papyrus marsh hurling a curved stick into a flock of birds, a scene of [[fowling]] that has survived for over three thousand years. Australian makers produced heavier [[club (weapon)|clubs]] and straight throwing sticks for close fighting and hunting, and these were built to strike rather than to curve.

## The one built to return

The returning kind is a specialised tool, most closely associated with Australia. Each arm is an [[airfoil|Airfoil]], cut like an aircraft wing with a flatter face and a curved one, and the two arms are joined so the whole piece spins as a unit. Makers carved them by hand and adjusted the arms by throwing and trimming until the flight curved back.

::: steps
Throw :: a flick of the wrist sends it spinning forward through the air
Spin :: each arm turns like a [[propeller|Propeller]] blade and works as a wing
Lift :: the arm moving faster through the air gets more lift
Tilt :: the spinning plane tips sideways through [[gyroscopic precession|Gyroscopic precession]]
Return :: the curved path brings it round toward the thrower
:::

A return is never guaranteed. Wind, a thrower's grip and the angle of release all change the path, so a throw can end short, wide or in the grass. The spin also gives the boomerang the steadiness of a [[gyroscope|Gyroscope]], which is why experienced throwers can watch the flight and step forward to catch it on the way back.

A well-thrown returning boomerang can circle back and land near the thrower, which is why it is the design most people picture. The return is also the hardest part to learn, because it can arrive at head height or dip toward the grass, and a beginner must read the spin and the wind before stepping into its path. Experienced throwers practise in open ground, where a missed catch costs nothing.

## Throwing aids and a word from Sydney

Other peoples gave a throw more leverage rather than more curve. The [[woomera (spear-thrower)|woomera]] of Australia and the [[atlatl]] of the Americas both extend the thrower's arm, so a spear leaves the hand faster, and a [[sling (weapon)|sling]] does something similar with a loop of cord. Each is a way of making a short arm throw further, while the boomerang takes a different route and asks the air to bring the stick back.

^ A woomera has a peg at one end that seats in a notch at the spear's butt, turning the thrower's arm into a longer lever.

The English word comes from Dharug, the language of the Sydney region. Today competitive throwers compete for distance, accuracy and trick catches, and most competition models are cut from plywood or moulded from plastic rather than carved from wood. The airfoil shape has changed less than the materials, so a modern throw still rides the same physics.
