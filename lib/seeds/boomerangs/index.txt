A boomerang is, at heart, a curved piece of wood that flies. The word comes from a Dharug-language term recorded near Sydney in the late 1700s, and it was later applied by Europeans to a whole family of bent throwing implements. The famous returning kind, with two slim arms set at an angle and a flight path that loops back to the thrower's feet, is the rarest member of that family. Far more numerous were the heavy, nearly straight [[throwing stick|throwing sticks]] that flew in flat arcs and never came home, the hunting weapons of [[Aboriginal Australians]] across the continent, and cousins of them used on other continents.

^ Oblazowa's boomerang was carved from a mammoth tusk, not wood, and may have been a thrown club rather than a returner.

## Weapons that stayed away

The oldest boomerang-like object yet found came from [[Oblazowa Cave]] in the Carpathian Mountains of Poland, excavated in 1987 and dated to roughly 23,000 years ago. It is a curved blade of [[mammoth ivory|mammoth tusk]], about 70 centimetres long, and experimenters who built replicas found that it could be thrown to fly a short loop. Whether its makers meant it to return is unknown; many researchers think it was a hunting club. In Australia, wooden throwing sticks were preserved in the peat of [[Wyrie Swamp]] in South Australia, dated to around 10,000 years ago, and some of those curved pieces show a shape that could fly back.

Non-returning boomerangs were built for killing. A hunter might hurl one into a flock of ducks or cockatoos on the ground, or into the legs of a kangaroo, where a spinning blade could break bone at 100 metres. Heavier forms served as war clubs, clap sticks for music, fire-starting saws, digging tools and ceremonial objects. The [[kylie (boomerang)|kylie]] of Western Australia is one such, a flat, slightly curved stick that skips and ricochets along the ground. Returning boomerangs seem to have been used chiefly for sport, for decoy hunting (a hawk-like whirring that made flocks bunch) and for display.

::: facts
Typical wood :: Mulga, gidgee, other dense hardwoods
Weapon throw range :: Up to about 100 metres
Returning throw range :: Roughly 20 to 40 metres
Common weapon forms :: Hunting stick, war boomerang, kylie
:::

## Why one comes back

A returning boomerang is a pair of rotating wings. Each arm is shaped like an airfoil, rounded on one face and flatter on the other, so it generates [[lift (force)|lift]] as it spins. A right-handed thrower launches it nearly upright, tilted slightly, spinning anticlockwise as seen from behind. Because the arm at the top moves faster relative to the air (spin speed plus forward speed) than the arm at the bottom (spin speed minus forward speed), it produces more lift, and the boomerang is pushed unevenly at its top edge.

That uneven push would simply tip a stationary object over, but a spinning one behaves like a gyroscope. The force acts, and the response arrives a quarter turn later: [[gyroscopic precession]] turns the spinning plane, so the boomerang leans over and curves in a wide arc rather than flipping. As the arc closes, the boomerang gradually lies flatter, and in a well-made throw it settles into a hovering spin above the thrower's hands. Small changes matter enormously: a gust, a wrongly angled launch or arms bent a few degrees off can send it elsewhere entirely.

::: steps
Launch :: Thrown nearly upright, spinning fast, aimed slightly above the horizon.
Uneven lift :: The upper arm moves faster through air than the lower one.
Precession :: Spin turns that push into a leftward lean of the whole plane.
Curve :: The path bends into a roughly circular loop.
Return :: The boomerang flattens and drops, ideally near the thrower.
:::

## Out of the Outback

Boomerangs turn up in unexpected places. Curved throwing sticks have been recorded among the Hopi of Arizona, in southern India and in ancient Egypt, where several non-returning examples were among the grave goods of [[Tutankhamun]], who died around 1323 BCE. [[Hieroglyphs]] show fowlers using such sticks in marsh hunts, a reminder that bird-hunting with a thrown club was an old and independent invention. Some of the king's pieces were made of wood, others of ivory or even decorated with gold.

In the 20th century boomerangs became a sport. Modern competitors throw lightweight birch-ply or composite boomerangs for accuracy, endurance (catching as many returns as possible in five minutes) and "trick catch", behind the back or under a leg. The [[Boomerang Throwing World Cup]] has been held since 1989, and the sport has its own maths of wind, weight and wing angle. The old weapon makers had solved a version of the same problem by touch, shaping the wood by eye until a curve that cut the air one way would carry it another.
