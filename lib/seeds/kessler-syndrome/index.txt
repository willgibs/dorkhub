In 1978, NASA scientist [[Donald J. Kessler]] and his colleague Burton Cour-Palais published a paper that asked an uncomfortable question: what happens when there is so much junk in orbit that it begins to make more junk by itself? Their answer was that past a certain density, collisions between objects would shatter them into fragments, and those fragments would collide with other objects, in a cascade that feeds on its own products. The resulting shell of debris could make some orbits unusable for generations. The phenomenon was later named after Kessler, though he did not coin the term himself.

The danger comes from speed. In [[Low Earth orbit]], objects travel at roughly 7.5 kilometres per second, so two satellites meeting at an angle can close at over 10 km/s, more than ten times the speed of a rifle bullet. At those energies, a one-centimetre fragment of aluminium can carry the punch of a hand grenade, and a paint fleck has cracked a space shuttle window. Spacecraft armour such as the [[Whipple shield]], a thin outer sheet that vaporizes the impactor before it reaches the hull, can stop millimetre-scale particles but not anything much larger.

^ A "collision" in orbit needs no head-on crash: debris in one orbit crosses another's path from a different angle, and the closing speed does the damage.

## The numbers behind the fear

The [[Space Surveillance Network]], run by the United States, catalogues objects larger than about ten centimetres, and the count of tracked pieces exceeds 30,000. Estimates for fragments between one and ten centimetres run to around a million, and for those over a millimetre, well over a hundred million. Most are too small to track but still lethal to a satellite.

Real collisions have turned theory into data. In 2007 China destroyed its own weather satellite, [[Fengyun-1C]], in an anti-satellite missile test, producing more than 3,000 trackable fragments in one of the most heavily used altitude bands. Then in February 2009 the defunct Russian [[Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251 collision|Cosmos 2251]] struck an operational Iridium communications satellite about 790 km above Siberia, the first known accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact satellites. Together these two events added thousands of tracked pieces, many of which will persist for decades.

## Why it is a slow-motion disaster

Kessler syndrome is not an explosion but a process that unfolds over years or centuries, and that is what makes it hard to see. The cleanup mechanism is [[Atmospheric drag]]: wisps of upper atmosphere slow orbiting objects until they sink and burn up, a process called [[Orbital decay]]. Below roughly 400 km this happens within years. Higher up, around 800 to 1,000 km, the air is so thin that debris can linger for centuries, which is why these altitudes are the most worrying. Fragments from a collision there are effectively permanent on human timescales.

The cascade is therefore a race between creation and decay. If launches and collisions add debris faster than drag removes it, the population grows exponentially, as in any [[Chain reaction]]. Some modelling suggests parts of low orbit may already be unstable, in the sense that collisions alone could keep growing the debris population even if all launching stopped, though this is debated and depends heavily on assumptions about future launches.

The popular image from the 2013 film Gravity, of a debris storm wiping out everything in minutes, is wrong in both speed and geometry. A real cascade would be gradual, concentrated in particular altitude shells, and would cost satellites one by one. Still, the arrival of mega-constellations such as [[Starlink]], with thousands of spacecraft, raises the stakes, and operators now rely on automated collision-avoidance manoeuvres and rapid deorbiting of dead satellites. Proposals for [[Active debris removal]], including nets, harpoons and robotic arms, aim to pluck out the largest derelicts before they collide, since a few hundred big objects are responsible for most of the future risk.

^ Kessler himself has said a cascade may already have begun in some altitude bands, but unfolding so slowly that it is hard to observe directly.
