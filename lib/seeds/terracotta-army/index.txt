Around 8,000 life-sized soldiers stand in battle formation in pits near the city of [[Xi'an]], facing east toward the lands their emperor conquered. They were made for [[Qin Shi Huang]], who united China in 221 BCE and, as soon as he took the throne of Qin as a boy of thirteen, began preparing his tomb. Work on the vast burial complex is said to have involved some 700,000 conscripts, a figure from the historian [[Sima Qian]] writing a century and a half later. The clay army is only a fringe of that complex: the emperor's burial mound itself, a few kilometres west, has never been opened.

On a March day in 1974, farmers digging a well in drought-struck Lintong district, Shaanxi, struck fragments of fired clay and bronze arrowheads. They had no idea they had found the largest known group of sculpture from the ancient world. Archaeologists followed, and over the next years excavation revealed three main pits (a fourth turned out to be empty, apparently abandoned unfinished). Pit 1 alone, roughly 230 metres long, holds the main infantry body in eleven corridors, and the soldiers are still being counted; only a fraction has been dug. Like [[Pompeii]], the site was found by accident and preserves a world frozen in the moment its makers abandoned it, though here the intent was ritual rather than catastrophe.

## Every face different
The soldiers were not cast from one mould. Workshops built bodies from coiled and stacked clay, with legs often solid and torsos hollow, then added hands, ears and heads from moulds before sculpting details by hand. The result is a startling variety of faces, hairstyles, beards and expressions, along with rank-specific armour: officers, archers, charioteers, cavalrymen, generals taller than common infantry. Many pieces carry stamped or incised names of workshop foremen and craftsmen, which suggests a system of accountability: faulty work could be traced to its maker.

^ Archaeologists have found more than eighty inscribed craftsman names on the figures, a quality-control system that tied each soldier back to a person or workshop.

The weapons were real. The bronze swords, spears and [[Crossbow|crossbows]] found in the pits were functional, and the arrowheads were machined to remarkably consistent tolerances. Some blades were found still sharp after more than two thousand years. Reports in the 1990s that they had been chrome-plated captured attention; later analysis pointed toward a protective surface layer produced by the alloy and the burial environment rather than deliberate [[Chromium plating|chromium plating]], though the exact cause is still discussed.

## Once in colour
Today the army is the colour of earth, but it was not made that way. Each figure was coated in a layer of [[Lacquer|lacquer]] from the sap of the lacquer tree, then painted in bright mineral pigments: reds, greens, blues, purples and a rare synthetic pigment known as Han purple (or Chinese purple), made from barium copper silicate. When the pits were first opened, the paint began to curl and flake within minutes as the lacquer dried in open air, leaving the bare clay seen in most photographs. Conservators have since developed ways to stabilise paint on freshly excavated pieces, and traces of colour survive on some figures. The effect recalls the way the [[Mona Lisa]] has changed with age: what we see is the work's long afterlife, not the surface its maker intended.

## The wider tomb
Sima Qian wrote that the emperor's burial chamber contained rivers of [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] with models of the heavens above and the land below. Soil tests at the burial mound in the 1980s found abnormally high mercury concentrations, lending some support to the account, though the chamber itself remains unexcavated and officials cite the need to protect it. Beyond the army, pits have yielded bronze birds, acrobats, musicians, stone armour made of thousands of limestone plates, and two half-scale bronze [[Chariot|chariots]] with working parts. The army itself was probably damaged soon after the dynasty fell, when rebel forces looted weapons and set fire to the timber roofs, collapsing them onto the figures below.

Why build it at all? The usual reading is that the army served the emperor in the afterlife, as a mirror of the force that had won him an empire. Earlier Chinese burials had used living sacrifices; replacing them with clay is sometimes argued to mark a shift, though that interpretation is debated.
