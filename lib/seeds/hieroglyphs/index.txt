Hieroglyphs are the formal writing of ancient Egypt: hundreds of small, exquisitely drawn pictures of birds, snakes, loaves, reeds and human figures, carved on temple walls and painted in tombs. Their name comes from Greek, "sacred carvings," because by the time Greeks visited Egypt the script was used mostly by priests. The earliest known signs, found on small bone and ivory labels in a tomb at Abydos, date to around 3250 BCE. The script then lasted over three thousand years, outliving the pharaohs, the Persians and the Ptolemies.

The great surprise is that hieroglyphs are not picture-writing. Most signs stand for sounds, much as letters do, and the script mixes three kinds of sign: phonograms (a mouth means "r"), logograms (a sun disc means "sun" or "day") and silent determinatives placed at the end of a word to hint at its category, such as a seated man for people or a papyrus roll for abstractions. Egyptian scribes wrote mainly consonants, leaving the vowels unrecorded, so we know the signs but must reconstruct how the language sounded. Writing could run left to right, right to left or in columns, and you tell which by facing the animals: they look toward the beginning of the line.

700 :: signs in common use in the Middle Kingdom
5,000 :: signs known in the Ptolemaic period

:::

## Why it was lost

Hieroglyphs had everyday cousins. Scribes wrote quickly in hieratic, a cursive form, and from about 650 BCE in [[Demotic script|Demotic]], a still more compressed script for contracts and letters. Monumental hieroglyphs stayed alive for religion and royal display, and the last dated inscription, at the temple of [[Philae]], was carved in 394 CE. Soon afterward the temples were closed under Christian emperors, the priestly class faded, and nobody was left who could read the signs. Egyptians themselves moved to writing [[Coptic language|Coptic]] in a Greek-derived alphabet.

For centuries the wrong ideas took hold. A late antique text attributed to [[Horapollo]] explained each sign as a symbolic riddle (a hare means "open" because its eyes never close), and Renaissance scholars built whole philosophies on this mystical reading. It was a dead end, since most signs simply spell sounds. The myth of an esoteric, secret wisdom in the signs even fed romantic notions of a lost super-civilisation; the Greek philosopher Plato, in the dialogues that launched the legend of [[Atlantis]], claimed his story came from Egyptian priests at Sais.

^ Around 1000 BCE Egyptian scribes were already mixing sound and meaning with a speed that modern puzzle designers would envy: the same sign could be spelled, sounded, or simply decorative.

## The decipherment

In 1799 French soldiers digging near Rashid (Rosetta) in the Nile Delta unearthed a granodiorite slab carrying one decree in three scripts: hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. The [[Rosetta Stone]] was a gift to scholarship, because the Greek could be read. The English polymath [[Thomas Young]] made the first real breakthroughs around 1814, seeing that the oval rings called [[Cartouche|cartouches]] enclosed royal names such as Ptolemy and that some signs were phonetic.

::: timeline
1799 :: Rosetta Stone found by French troops
1814 :: Young identifies phonetic values in cartouches
1822 :: Champollion's Lettre à M. Dacier announces the system
1824 :: Champollion publishes his Précis of the hieroglyphic system
1828 :: Champollion travels to Egypt and reads monuments on site
:::

[[Jean-François Champollion]], a Grenoble-born linguist who had learned Coptic as a teenager, took the decisive step. Comparing the cartouche of Ptolemy with one from Philae containing Cleopatra, he matched shared letters. Then a cartouche from Abu Simbel showed a sun disc and two s-signs; because Coptic ra meant "sun," he read Ra-m-s-s, Ramesses. Egyptian, in other words, was behind the script: ancient words that Coptic still preserved. That insight, a kind of [[Known-plaintext attack|known-plaintext attack]], resembled how codebreakers later cracked messages by guessing predictable words, and announced the whole system in 1822.

Because the script is a [[Rebus|rebus]] at heart, every sign had to be worked out: the same technique that gave the world the pun gave Egypt its writing. The script even left its trace on the alphabet; the [[Proto-Sinaitic script]] of miners in Sinai around 1850 BCE borrowed Egyptian pictures to write a Semitic language, and from it come the letters you are reading. And Egyptian texts opened with Champollion run from the [[Book of the Dead]] to tomb lists that mention, among royal objects, throwing sticks like the [[Boomerangs]] found in Tutankhamun's tomb.
