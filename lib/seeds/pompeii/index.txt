Stone ruts still score the paving of Pompeii's main streets, worn by carts that once rolled past its shops. Walk the streets and the colour survives too: red and yellow walls, mosaic floors and bakery ovens still in place. Perhaps 11,000 people lived here on the plain below Vesuvius, near the Bay of Naples, when the mountain buried it.

## How the mountain buried the town

The mountain was [[Vesuvius|Volcanoes]], barely more than a kilometre high, and modern estimates put its column of ash and pumice at up to 33 km. [[Pliny the Younger]] watched the cloud climb from across the bay, some 29 km away, and in letters written decades later likened it to a pine tree, a tall trunk spreading into branches at the top. [[Pumice|Pumice (rock)]], a rock so frothy that it floats, fell on the town eight kilometres away and heaped up in its streets for hours. Then came the [[pyroclastic surges|Pyroclastic flow]], fast clouds of hot gas and rock that rolled down the slopes and reached the town, killing many of those who had stayed. The surges also buried the smaller seaside town of [[Herculaneum|Herculaneum (ancient town)]] under deeper, hotter deposits.
^ Vesuvius was not seen as dangerous: its fertile slopes were farmed and planted with vines.

Textbooks date the eruption to 24 August AD 79, a date taken from Pliny's letters, but that date may be months off. A graffito found in Pompeii gives 17 October, and scholars still argue over which date the evidence supports.

## Hollows in the ash

Pompeii came back to light the way many buried places do, by accident, while someone dug for something else. In 1974 farmers digging a well for water near Xi'an struck the [[Terracotta Army]], a buried army of clay that had waited more than two thousand years.

The most haunting results at Pompeii are the casts of the dead. Bodies decayed inside the hardened ash, leaving hollows that kept each person's shape. Pouring liquid plaster into those cavities, a method known as [[plaster casting|Plaster casting (archaeology)]], produced figures frozen in the postures of their last moments. The technique is credited to [[Giuseppe Fiorelli]], who recognised the hollows as human shapes.

## What the ash kept

The ash kept the everyday better than the grand. Painted notices still ask passers-by to vote for local candidates, and the walls are scrawled with insults and love notes. A [[thermopolium|Thermopolium]], a Roman fast-food counter, had jars sunk into its stone top to hold food and drink, and such counters lined the busy streets. The walls show the [[four Pompeian styles|Pompeian styles (wall painting)]] of Roman painting: the earliest imitate marble blocks, while later ones paint windows onto imaginary architecture. And one room in the [[Villa of the Mysteries]] holds a long painted frieze of an initiation rite, whose meaning is still argued over.

Pompeii could also be violent. Tacitus records a brawl at a gladiator show in the amphitheatre in AD 59, between Pompeians and rivals from nearby Nuceria, which became a [[riot|Pompeii amphitheatre riot (AD 59)]] and cost the town its games for ten years.
^ The amphitheatre, built around 70 BC, was about 150 years old when Vesuvius erupted.
