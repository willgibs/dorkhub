Pompeii was a prosperous Roman town of perhaps 11,000 to 12,000 people, set on a lava-built rise a few kilometres from the sea and in the shadow of [[Mount Vesuvius]], a mountain its inhabitants did not know was a volcano. It had a forum, two theatres, an amphitheatre, some 30 bakeries, and street-corner snack counters whose sunken jars once held hot food. In AD 79 the mountain erupted and the town vanished under pumice and ash, preserved so completely that walls still carry election slogans, insults and a pregnant woman's graffito-sized shopping lists. Rediscovered in the 18th century, it is the most intimate view of everyday Roman life that survives.

The eruption is known from an eyewitness. [[Pliny the Younger]], then seventeen, watched from [[Misenum]], about 29 km across the Bay of Naples, and described the cloud rising "like an umbrella pine": a trunk shooting upward, then spreading into branches. Modern volcanologists reconstruct the column at roughly 33 km high, and the style of eruption now bears his name: a [[Plinian eruption]]. His uncle [[Pliny the Elder]], commander of the fleet, sailed toward the disaster to attempt rescues and died on the shore at Stabiae.

^ The Plinian column threw out material at an estimated rate of over a million tonnes per second at its peak, and the eruption lasted about 18 hours.

## Two days, two deaths

Pompeii lay downwind. For roughly the first 18 hours, white then grey [[Pumice]] fell on the town, piling up to about 2.8 m, enough to collapse roofs but leaving time to flee, and most of the population probably did. Then the column began to collapse, and in the early hours of the second day successive [[Pyroclastic flow|pyroclastic surges]] raced down the mountain at speeds of many tens of kilometres per hour. The nearer town of [[Herculaneum]] was hit first, by surges so hot that they carbonised wood and, in a 2020 study of skeletons in its boat houses, appear to have vaporised body fluids. At Pompeii the surges were cooler but lethal; about 1,100 bodies have been recovered, though the true death toll is unknown.

::: steps
Opening blast :: Steam-driven explosions clear the vent; a dark column climbs over Vesuvius.
Pumice fall :: Fall-out blankets Pompeii for hours, building up metres of lapilli.
Column collapse :: The overloaded plume falls back under its own weight.
Surges :: Avalanches of hot gas and ash sweep the town in minutes.
Ash burial :: Fine ash and rain-washed mud seal streets, rooms and bodies.
:::

## The body casts

Ash hardened around the dead, and as bodies decayed they left hollows. In 1863 [[Giuseppe Fiorelli]], who directed the excavations, realised what these voids were and had liquid plaster poured into them, recovering the final postures of victims: a man covering his face, a huddled child. Fiorelli also introduced the division of the town into numbered regions and blocks that archaeologists still use. Recent work has used CT scanning of the casts and DNA to show that some pairs thought to be mothers and children were in fact unrelated, a reminder that the old stories were partly storytelling.

## Was it really 24 August?

The traditional date comes from manuscripts of Pliny the Younger's letters, which are medieval copies and may contain scribal errors. Several finds hint at autumn: charcoal graffiti dated to "the sixteenth day before the Kalends of November" (about 17 October), braziers in use, pomegranates and autumn fruits, and heavier clothing. In 2018 an inscription in a house on Region V, written in charcoal, was read as 17 October, and many scholars now favour an October or later date, though the question is not closed.

## What the town shows

Pompeii's evidence is the ordinary. Its street slabs are rutted by cart wheels; its shops sold [[Garum]], the fermented fish sauce; the [[Cave canem mosaic]] warns visitors of the dog; and the [[Villa of the Mysteries]] holds a huge red-walled fresco of an unknown initiation rite. The town also reveals the scale of the damage that came before: a major earthquake in AD 62 had left much of it still under repair. Even the buildings tell the story, since [[Roman concrete]] and its volcanic ash ingredient were part of how the city was made.
