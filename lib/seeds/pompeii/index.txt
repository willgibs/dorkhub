Pompeii was a prosperous Roman port town of perhaps 10,000 to 12,000 people, set on the Bay of Naples about ten kilometres from [[Vesuvius]]. Its streets were paved with huge basalt blocks, worn into ruts by cart wheels, and crossed by raised stepping stones so pedestrians could keep their sandals out of the runoff. Shops opened straight onto the pavement, walls carried election slogans and graffiti, and bakeries, fullers' workshops and wine bars lined the main roads. Then, in AD 79, the town was buried so quickly that it kept its bread, its insults and its people exactly where the eruption found them.

Today it is one of the few places where an entire ancient town can be walked from end to end. Inside the circuit of its walls, roughly two-thirds of the area has been dug out of the ash and pumice. The remaining third, mostly in the northeast, has never been excavated, and on a map of the site it simply shows as unbroken ground. That is deliberate as much as practical: archaeologists now argue that leaving a portion buried is a gift to people with better methods than ours.

## The afternoon it ended

The eruption began around midday with a column of gas and pumice that rose high above the mountain, a textbook [[Plinian eruption]]. Pompeii lay downwind, and for hours pumice fell on the town, piling up to about three metres. Roofs collapsed under the load, but many people could still have escaped on foot or by road. The best eyewitness account comes from [[Pliny the Younger]], who watched from across the bay at Misenum and wrote it down decades later in letters to the historian Tacitus.

During the night and early morning, the column collapsed and sent [[Pyroclastic flow|pyroclastic flows]] and surges racing down the slopes. These avalanches of hot gas and ash reached Pompeii within minutes and killed those still sheltering there. Studies of the bodies suggest death came from the heat and the ash-laden air, not slow suffocation as once assumed. Nearby [[Herculaneum]] met the same surges earlier and was sealed under a deeper, harder layer.

^ The exact date is contested. Pliny's manuscripts give 24 August, but a charcoal graffito found in 2018 mentions a date in October, and many scholars now favour autumn.

## Digging a town out

Excavations began under the Bourbon rulers of Naples in 1748, and the early digs were treasure hunts: statues and paintings were cut out and carted to royal collections. Method arrived with [[Giuseppe Fiorelli]], who took charge in 1863. He divided the town into numbered regions, blocks and doorways, a system still used to cite any address in Pompeii. He also noticed that the ash had hardened around vanished bodies, leaving hollows in the shape of the dead.

::: steps
Notice :: Workers strike hollows in the hardened ash layer.
Pour :: Liquid plaster is poured into the void through a small opening.
Set :: The plaster hardens inside the cavity left by the body.
Reveal :: The ash is chipped away, leaving a cast of the victim's final posture.
:::

That [[Plaster cast (Pompeii)|plaster cast]] technique gave the town its most haunting images, such as a dog straining against its chain and families huddled together. Newer work uses CT scans of the casts, which reveal teeth, bones and sometimes surprising details about the people inside.

## Everyday Rome, preserved

Pompeii matters less for its disasters than for how ordinary it was. A street-corner [[Thermopolium]] with painted jars in its counter served hot food to passers-by. Workshops produced [[Garum]], the fermented fish sauce Romans loved, and its trade is traced in surviving amphorae. Rooms of the [[Villa of the Mysteries]] hold a vast [[Fresco]] cycle whose meaning, probably a Dionysiac initiation, is still debated. At the [[Amphitheatre of Pompeii]], one of the oldest surviving stone amphitheatres, a brawl between Pompeians and visitors from nearby Nuceria in AD 59 got the venue banned for ten years, according to Tacitus.

^ The same region is not finished: [[Campi Flegrei]], a caldera just west of Naples, sits under about 500,000 people, and its ground is rising again.

Because the ash fell as a sealed blanket, even fragile things survived: loaves of bread, wooden shutters, carbonised food. For anyone studying how a [[Volcanoes|volcano]] can freeze a society in a single afternoon, nowhere else offers a record this complete.
