Antarctica is the fifth-largest continent, around 14 million square kilometres, and about 98 percent of it lies under ice. That ice is the [[Antarctic ice sheet]], which averages roughly 2 kilometres thick and reaches about 4.8 kilometres in places. It holds around 90 percent of the planet's ice and roughly 70 percent of its fresh water. If it all melted, the seas would rise by about 58 metres, enough to drown most of the world's coastal cities; no one expects that, but even small losses from its margins matter.

The continent is the coldest, driest and windiest on Earth. The interior is technically a desert, receiving only a few centimetres of precipitation a year in water equivalent, and snow that falls near the South Pole may never melt. In July 1983 the Soviet Vostok station recorded -89.2 °C, the lowest air temperature ever measured directly at ground level. Along the coast, [[katabatic wind|katabatic winds]] pour off the high plateau, driven by dense cold air sliding downhill, and at Commonwealth Bay they have been clocked averaging far above gale force for days.

^ The ice is heavy enough to push the bedrock beneath it down by hundreds of metres in places; if it vanished, the land would slowly rebound upward.

## Ice that remembers

Because snow in the interior is never thawed, each year's fall is stacked on the last, trapping tiny bubbles of ancient air. The [[Ice core|ice cores]] drilled by the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica at Dome C reached back about 800,000 years, recording eight glacial cycles of the [[Ice ages]] and the carbon dioxide levels that rose and fell with them. Nothing in that record approaches today's concentration of the gas. Beneath the ice lies [[Lake Vostok]], one of more than 400 subglacial lakes, kept liquid by geothermal heat and the insulating weight above, and sealed off for possibly millions of years.

Ice also preserves rocks from space. Dark meteorites stand out on the pale surface, and glacial flow concentrates them where it is stopped against mountains, such as at the [[Allan Hills meteorites|Allan Hills]]. Allan Hills A81005, found in 1982, was identified as a piece of the Moon, with a composition matching samples brought back by [[Apollo 11]] and its successors. Antarctica has since yielded tens of thousands of meteorites, more than the rest of the world combined.

## A continent run by treaty

Exploration of the interior came late. [[Roald Amundsen]] beat Robert Falcon Scott to the Pole by about five weeks in 1911, and Scott's party died on the return. [[Ernest Shackleton]] turned a failed expedition into a legend when his ship Endurance was crushed in the Weddell Sea ice, and he led all 27 of his men to safety in 1916 after a boat journey to South Georgia. Seven countries had territorial claims, some overlapping. The [[Antarctic Treaty]] of 1959 froze them, banned military activity, and reserved the continent for science; it now has more than 50 parties.

Life clings to the margins. The [[Emperor penguin]] breeds through the winter on sea ice, males incubating a single egg on their feet for about two months in temperatures far below freezing. In the [[Dry Valleys]], almost snowless and ice-free, the climate is so Mars-like that NASA tested spacecraft there, and microbes survive inside porous rocks. Above, during the long polar night, the southern lights, a counterpart of the [[Northern lights]], ripple over the ice.

Antarctica is also where the [[Ozone hole]] was found: each southern spring, chlorine from human-made chemicals, working on the surface of polar stratospheric clouds, strips ozone from the air above it. The 1987 Montreal Protocol cut those emissions, and the hole is slowly recovering.

^ No one lives there permanently, but about 1,000 people winter at research stations, and the summer population rises to roughly 5,000.
