DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, is a long chain molecule that stores the instructions for building and running an organism. Its alphabet has only four letters, the bases adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine, yet the sequence of roughly 3.1 billion of these pairs in a human cell specifies everything from haemoglobin to eye colour. Unwound, the DNA in one of your cells would stretch about 2 metres, and the whole of it is packed into a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre across. Laid end to end, the DNA from all the cells in one body would reach to the Sun and back many times over.

## Two strands, one logic

Each strand is a backbone of alternating sugar and phosphate groups, with a base attached to every sugar. Two strands wind around each other into the [[double helix|Double helix]], held together by hydrogen bonds between bases: A always pairs with T, and G always with C. This pairing rule follows from [[Chargaff's rules|Chargaff's rules]], Erwin Chargaff's 1950 finding that the amounts of A and T in a sample match, as do G and C. The helix is only about 2 nanometres wide, with one full turn every 10 or so base pairs.

The pairing is the whole trick. Pull the strands apart and each one carries enough information to rebuild its partner, which is how a cell copies its genome before dividing. Copying is astonishingly accurate: proofreading and repair enzymes hold errors to roughly one in a billion bases copied, and the rare mistakes that survive are the raw material of mutation and evolution.

^ Miescher first isolated DNA in 1869 from pus on discarded surgical bandages, calling it "nuclein". Nobody thought it carried heredity for another 75 years.

## Finding the shape

In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty showed that DNA, not protein, carried heredity in bacteria. The race to its structure then peaked in Cambridge and London. [[Rosalind Franklin]], working at King's College London with her student Raymond Gosling, produced [[Photo 51|Photo 51]] in 1952, an X-ray diffraction image whose X-shaped pattern pointed to a helix. Her data, shown to James Watson without her knowledge, helped Watson and Francis Crick build their metal-plate model in March 1953. Their paper, barely a page long, closed with the understatement that the pairing "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism". Watson, Crick and Maurice Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize; Franklin had died in 1958.

## Packing, reading, using

Two metres of thread fits in a nucleus because DNA is wound around spools of proteins called [[histones|Histone]], then coiled again into the dense bundles of a [[chromosome|Chromosome]]. Humans carry 46 chromosomes, and only about 1 to 2 percent of the sequence codes for proteins. Cells also carry a second, small genome in the [[mitochondria|Mitochondrial DNA]], inherited almost entirely from the mother.

Reading DNA became routine through [[Sanger sequencing|Sanger sequencing]], devised by Frederick Sanger in 1977, and the [[polymerase chain reaction|Polymerase chain reaction]], conceived by Kary Mullis in 1983, which copies a stretch of DNA millions of times from a trace sample. The [[Human Genome Project|Human Genome Project]] used the former to assemble the first human reference sequence, announced in 2003 after 13 years. Today a genome can be sequenced in hours for a few hundred dollars.

The molecule has since escaped biology. Variable repeats in everyone's DNA underpin [[genetic fingerprinting|Genetic fingerprinting]], introduced by Alec Jeffreys in Leicester in 1984. Strands can be folded into nanoscale smiley faces and boxes as [[DNA origami|DNA origami]], a method Paul Rothemund published in 2006. And fragments recovered from bones thousands of years old, the field of [[ancient DNA|Ancient DNA]], have rewritten human history, even tracing the ancestry of the people who raised [[Stonehenge]].

^ Identical twins share the same genome, so DNA profiling cannot easily tell them apart, though their fingerprints still differ.
