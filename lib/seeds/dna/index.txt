Take a typical cell from your body, unwind all of its DNA and join the strands end to end, and the result runs about two metres. It is packed into a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre across and written in four chemical letters: A, C, G and T. Nearly every living thing uses this same molecule, so a bacterium, a sunflower and a blue whale keep their instructions in one alphabet.

^ On average any two humans share about 99.9 per cent of their DNA letters, which leaves roughly three million places where they differ.

## A ladder twisted into a thread

The molecule is a double helix: two strands wound like the rails of a twisted ladder. Each rung pairs A with T, held by two hydrogen bonds, or G with C, held by three. The rungs stack about 0.34 nanometres apart and the helix completes a turn roughly every ten rungs, keeping the whole molecule about 2 nanometres wide. The shape was pieced together from X-ray images, among them [[Photo 51]], taken in 1952 in [[Rosalind Franklin]]'s laboratory at King's College London.

Those rungs add up to the two metres. One copy of the human genome holds about 3.1 billion base pairs, and at 0.34 nanometres each that comes to roughly a metre. Most of your cells carry two copies, one from each parent, which doubles the length to about 2 metres. Together those copies are packed into 46 [[chromosomes|Chromosome]], each a single unbroken molecule, and the longest, number 1, carries about 250 million base pairs.

## From letters to proteins

Genes are the stretches of that sequence that code for products, mostly proteins. A gene is first copied into RNA, and a [[ribosome|Ribosome]] reads that copy three letters at a time. The [[genetic code|Genetic code]] matches each three-letter codon to one of 20 amino acids or a stop signal. The code is nearly identical in bacteria, fungi and people, which is why bacteria can be engineered to make human insulin.

Protein-coding sequence fills only about 1 to 2 per cent of the genome. Close to half is made of [[transposable elements|Transposable element]], sequences that hop around the genome, first identified in maize by Barbara McClintock in the 1940s. Chemical marks add another layer: [[DNA methylation]] attaches a methyl group to some C letters, which can silence a gene without changing its sequence.

^ Your liver and your neurons carry almost exactly the same DNA; what differs is which parts are switched on.

## Copying and reading

Before a cell divides, its double helix unzips and a team of enzymes builds a new partner against each strand. The [[Meselson–Stahl experiment]] of 1958 showed that each daughter keeps one old strand, by tracking heavy nitrogen through generations of bacteria. After proofreading and repair, a cell miscopies only about one base in a billion. Rare slips still get through, and they are the raw material of evolution.

The [[polymerase chain reaction|Polymerase chain reaction]], devised by Kary Mullis in 1983, doubles a DNA sample with every heating and cooling cycle, so about thirty cycles make a billion copies. Old DNA is fragmented and easily contaminated, yet in 2010 Svante Pääbo's team published a draft Neanderthal genome. It showed that 1 to 4 per cent of the DNA of people outside Africa came from [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]], through interbreeding tens of thousands of years ago.

## Identity, and what people build

Identical twins grow from one fertilised egg and share nearly all their DNA, yet their [[fingerprints|Fingerprints]] differ, because conditions in the womb shape the ridges too. [[DNA profiling]], which Alec Jeffreys developed at Leicester in 1984, reads short repeats that differ strongly between people. Paul Rothemund at Caltech folded DNA itself into shapes in 2006, a technique he called [[DNA origami|Origami]].
