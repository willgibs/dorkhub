Stand on the Greenland ice sheet and the land has vanished under ice up to about three kilometres thick. [[Antarctica]] holds more, in places over four kilometres deep, and both sheets are the surviving proof that Earth is still in an ice age. Each year's snow packs into a new layer, so the ice works as a diary of the air it fell through.

## What starts and stops an ice age

In 1837 [[Louis Agassiz]], a Swiss naturalist, argued that ice had once covered much of Europe, pointing to [[glacial erratics|Glacial erratic]] carried far from their mountains and to scratches cut into bedrock. The evidence held up, and by the twentieth century geologists had traced glacial deposits across much of Europe and North America. What his evidence could not explain was the rhythm, the way ice sheets came and went in cycles.

The timing was explained, in outline, by [[Milutin Milanković]], a Serbian engineer who worked out how Earth's orbit shifts over tens of thousands of years, the [[Milankovitch cycles]] that now bear his name. The orbital pattern was tested in 1976 by James Hays, John Imbrie and Nicholas Shackleton, who matched the cycles to the ice volume recorded in deep-sea sediment cores. The response is lopsided: ice sheets build slowly over many millennia and collapse far faster.

::: steps
Orbit :: Its shape and tilt shift, so high-latitude summers get less sunlight.
Snow :: Winter snow survives the summer instead of melting away.
Albedo :: Bright snow reflects more sunlight, so the land cools further.
Ice :: Growing ice sheets lock up seawater and lower the sea.
Carbon :: Atmospheric carbon dioxide falls, which deepens the cold.
:::

## When the sea fell

Water locked up in ice has to come from somewhere. At the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], roughly 20,000 years ago, global sea level stood about 120 metres lower than it does today. The North Sea was largely dry land, and the low plain now called [[Doggerland]] tied Britain to the continent. Its marshes and hunting grounds drowned as the ice melted, and trawlers still bring up mammoth bones from the seabed.

^ At the height of the last ice age, you could have walked from Britain to the Netherlands across land now under the sea.

[[Coral reefs]] keep a record of those shifts. Corals build only in shallow, sunlit water, so a reef marks the sea surface where it grew. On Barbados, fossil coral terraces, lifted by the slowly rising land, preserve old shorelines like steps on a staircase.

## Reading the ice

Drilling teams have pulled ice cores from the Antarctic plateau, one reaching back about 800,000 years. In 2004 the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica reported a core from Dome C that spans roughly eight glacial cycles. Dating the layers depends on counting annual snowfall near the surface and on ice-flow models deeper down.

Air bubbles sealed in each [[ice core|Ice core]] are samples of the atmosphere from the moment they closed, and their carbon dioxide rises and falls in step with the temperature recorded in the same ice. That match suggests carbon dioxide does more than follow the cold: it helps amplify it. Carbon dioxide is one link in a longer chain of feedbacks, and the order of the others is still debated.

^ Glacial-age carbon dioxide sat near 180 parts per million, against roughly 280 in the warm spells before industry.

Greenland's ice shows that the last ice age was no steady deep freeze. Its cores record repeated sudden warmings, the [[Dansgaard–Oeschger event|Dansgaard–Oeschger events]], first identified in Greenland ice by Willi Dansgaard and Hans Oeschger, in which the air above the ice warmed by many degrees within a few decades. Each warm burst was followed by a slide back toward cold, so the climate of that age kept lurching rather than settling.

The most recent sudden lurch came as the ice was already retreating. The [[Younger Dryas]] began about 12,900 years ago, returned parts of northern Europe to glacial cold for roughly 1,200 years, and ended abruptly. Its trigger is still disputed: the leading idea is a meltwater surge from [[Lake Agassiz]], the glacial lake that filled central North America, while a contested impact hypothesis has its own advocates.
