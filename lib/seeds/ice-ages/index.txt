By the strict geological definition, an ice age is any stretch of time when permanent ice sheets cover large parts of the planet, and by that measure we are living in one. Greenland and [[Antarctica]] still hold ice sheets, which together lock up about 2 percent of Earth's water and enough to raise the oceans by some 65 metres if they melted. The thing that feels like "the Ice Age", with mammoths and glaciers over Chicago, was only the latest cold pulse inside this longer age. Scientists call those pulses glacials, and the warm gaps between them interglacials. Our own warm spell, the Holocene, is an interglacial that began about 11,700 years ago.

The present age, the [[Quaternary glaciation]], began about 2.6 million years ago, when ice sheets started to build on northern continents. Since then the climate has swung back and forth, and for the last million years or so the beat has been roughly 100,000 years: long, slow glaciations, then quick thaws. At the peak, the [[Laurentide Ice Sheet]] smothered most of Canada and reached into the American Midwest, and was up to about 3 kilometres thick over Hudson Bay. ^ The weight of that ice pressed the crust down. Parts of Canada and Scandinavia are still rising today, a slow recovery known as [[Isostatic rebound]].

## What drives the cycle

The pacing comes from small, regular wobbles in Earth's orbit. The Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković spent years during the First World War and after working out how changes in the shape of the orbit, the tilt of the axis and the precession of the equinoxes alter summer sunshine at high northern latitudes. His [[Milankovitch cycles]] were dismissed for decades, then confirmed in 1976 when Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton found the same rhythms in deep-sea sediment. The orbital nudge is tiny, though, so feedbacks do the heavy lifting: ice reflects sunlight, and cold oceans absorb more carbon dioxide. Ice cores show CO2 falling to about 180 parts per million in glacial times and rising to about 280 in interglacials.

::: steps
Orbit shifts :: Summer sunshine at northern high latitudes weakens over thousands of years.
Snow persists :: Winter snow fails to melt in summer and piles up into ice.
Albedo rises :: White ice reflects more sunlight, cooling the surface further.
CO2 drops :: Colder oceans absorb more carbon dioxide, deepening the cooling.
Ice sheets grow :: Sea level falls and glaciers advance until the orbit turns back.
:::

## The world at the bottom

At the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], about 20,000 years ago, so much water was frozen that the sea stood roughly 120 metres lower than now. The shelf between Siberia and Alaska became [[Beringia]], a grassland a thousand kilometres wide that people and animals crossed, and the North Sea floor was dry [[Doggerland]]. The [[Coral reefs]] we admire today grew on submerged flanks of land; the glacial reefs sit drowned and stranded, and drilling off Tahiti and Barbados has used them to date the rising sea. Across Eurasia and North America stretched the [[Mammoth steppe]], a cold, dry, productive grassland that fed mammoths, woolly rhinos and bison.

The end of the glacial was not smooth. Around 12,900 years ago the climate lurched back toward cold for about 1,200 years in the [[Younger Dryas]], and the cause is still argued, with meltwater flooding the North Atlantic the leading suspect. Ice dams burst too: in the Pacific Northwest, [[Missoula floods]] repeatedly emptied a lake the size of Lake Ontario and Lake Erie combined across Washington, carving the Channeled Scablands.

## Deeper time

Quaternary ice is mild compared with the worst episodes. Geologists propose that around 700 million years ago the planet froze nearly or entirely, a [[Snowball Earth]] whose reality and severity are still debated. Antarctic ice preserves the more recent record: the [[EPICA]] core at Dome C reached back about 800,000 years, showing eight full glacial cycles in trapped air bubbles. Whether the next glaciation is delayed by the carbon dioxide humans have released is a live question, and several studies suggest it might be postponed for tens of thousands of years.
